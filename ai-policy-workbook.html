Part I

How to Use This Workbook

Each annotated policy entry in Part II applies one four-step analytical procedure and the three-level literacy framework. Apply the same procedure to new documents in Part III.

The Analytical Procedure

Four moves for reading any AI governance document, applied in sequence.

1

Identify the genre

What type of document is this? Who issued it, and in what institutional capacity? Genre sets the authority a document carries and the limits of what it is able to require.

2

Locate the glitch

Where does the document strain, contradict itself, or hedge where it claims to govern? At the glitch, the document's work becomes visible.

3

Read the reveal

What does the glitch expose about what the institution values, fears, or cannot resolve? What structural condition produced this document?

4

Name the abstraction

What general claim about AI governance, authorship, or power does this document support? What principle would guide better policy at this institution?

The Three-Level Framework

After applying the four-step procedure, characterize the literacy demands the document makes, or fails to make, at three levels. The framework page sets out the levels as the order in which a person reasons a decision (describe, analyze, decide). Here the same three levels read a document: what it asks its readers to recognize, to understand about the system, and to judge.

Conceptual

Recognition and naming: what terms does the policy use, and what do those terms assume? What are students and faculty able to name, and what goes unnamed?

Structural

Power and infrastructure: whose interests does the governance structure serve? Who authored this document, and what institutional pressures shaped it?

Operative

Contextual judgment: what operative choices does the policy ask faculty and students to make? What decision-making capacity does it cultivate or foreclose?

Reading for the Glitch

Every AI governance document produced under institutional pressure reveals something its authors did not intend. We call this the glitch: the moment where the document's language breaks down, hedges, contradicts itself, or produces an unintended meaning.

Treat the glitch as an analytical resource. It shows what the institution values and what it fears. It also marks what the authors were unable to resolve at the time of writing.

Look for: hedging language ("strongly discouraged"), contradictions between sections, silences where governance should be explicit, borrowed frameworks applied beyond their scope, and temporal artifacts (language accurate when written and no longer true).

Part II

Annotated Policy Corpus

This section presents six annotated policy entries selected from the 80-document corpus. Two entries treat NC State documents as a depth case. Four present comparative institutional examples. Each entry applies the four-step analytical procedure and the three-level literacy framework.

These annotations model one reading among several. Use them as a starting point for your own. Discussion notes at the end of each entry suggest how to open the annotation to disagreement.

NC State Depth Case · Entries 1–2

"any attempt to shortcut that training (such as using AI to write an essay) is fundamentally cheating their future selves"

Four-Step Analysis

1
Genre

Faculty newsletter: an internal advisory document from the Office for Faculty Excellence. The academic policy office did not issue it. It carries persuasive authority and has no enforcement mechanism.

2
Glitch

The document speaks with the certainty of institutional policy while it defers, explicitly, to faculty discretion. It issues a moral claim ("fundamentally cheating their future selves") and no rule.

3
Reveal

The institution does not know who is responsible for AI governance. The OFE newsletter fills the vacancy with moral rhetoric where policy infrastructure is missing.

4
Abstraction

When governance infrastructure is absent, moral rhetoric performs its function. The performance reveals the absence.

Three-Level Literacy Read

Conceptual

The document introduces no AI-specific vocabulary. "AI" and "generative AI" are used interchangeably without definition.

Structural

The document encodes faculty discretion as the operative governance mechanism. A faculty development office authored it, outside the academic policy structure, a structural mismatch.

Operative

The document offers instructors no decision framework. "Faculty discretion" is both the governance solution and the governance problem.

Discussion Notes

Use this entry to open a discussion of how advisory documents differ from policy documents. Ask: what would it take for this document to become policy? Who would have to issue it, and in what form?
Three sample statements, from “Highly Restricting AI Use in a Course” to “AI Use as a Collaborative Tool,” with questions to consider and no criteria for choosing among them.

Four-Step Analysis

1
Genre

Instructor resource guide: a practical toolkit from the teaching technology center. It models syllabus options without endorsing any of them.

2
Glitch

The three-tier model presents three mutually incompatible theoretical positions as equivalent options. An instructor who chooses restriction or encouragement makes an epistemological commitment. The document treats the choice as a matter of style.

3
Reveal

The model shows an institution settling the question of AI governance by declining to settle it. Institutionalized flexibility is institutionalized incoherence.

4
Abstraction

A resource presenting incompatible positions as equivalent choices hands the decision to the least-resourced actor, the individual instructor.

Three-Level Literacy Read

Conceptual

The DELTA document supplies concrete vocabulary for the three tiers (restrict, learning aid, collaborative tool). It offers the clearest conceptual framework in NC State's Wave 1 corpus.

Structural

DELTA, a teaching technology center, authored the document. It carries pedagogical authority and lacks governance authority. This structural gap is the glitch.

Operative

DELTA designed the document to support instructor decisions and supplied no criteria for making them. Instructors receive three options and no framework for choosing.

Discussion Notes

Pair with the OFE newsletter. Together they represent NC State's full Wave 1 institutional response: moral rhetoric (OFE) plus neutral options (DELTA). Ask: what would a university-wide policy provide, beyond what both documents offer?

Comparative Institutions · Entries 3–6

A July 2023 faculty committee report recommends three course-policy options (prohibit, allow with attribution, encourage and integrate) and discourages automatic AI detection. The Center for Teaching Innovation later published seven downloadable syllabus icons, from AI-FREE to ANY-AI.

Four-Step Analysis

1
Genre

Teaching resource with visual design components: a CTI toolkit with downloadable assets for instructors to embed in syllabi. The icon system converts policy into communication design.

2
Glitch

Cornell converts governance into a set of downloadable icons. Enforcement then depends entirely on instructors who deploy and explain those icons consistently. The icon is the policy. The icon has no enforcement mechanism.

3
Reveal

The icon system recasts governance as a problem of communication more than of rule-making. Cornell has answered the governance question by making it legible. Legibility and enforceability are separate achievements.

4
Abstraction

When enforcement is absent, governance becomes communication design. Design clarifies a position and leaves it uninstitutionalized.

Three-Level Literacy Read

Conceptual

The icon system works well as a conceptual literacy tool. It gives students a visual vocabulary for reading different AI use permissions at a glance.

Structural

The CTI authored the system. Neither the academic policy office nor the faculty senate issued it. The icons carry no institutional authority, and their effect depends entirely on voluntary adoption.

Operative

The committee report’s recommendation against automatic detection tools is the most consequential operative move. It moves the governance burden from policing to communication. The shift bears directly on how instructors design assignments. CWSP has taken the same position, with its reasons on the record.

Discussion Notes

The corpus offers no clearer example of governance performed as design. Use it to discuss how legibility relates to enforceability. Ask: does a visually clear policy become more or less likely to function as governance?
Every undergraduate beginning with the Class of 2029 required to graduate AI fluent, with AI fluency embedded across the curriculum.

Four-Step Analysis

1
Genre

Policy mandate: an Office of Academic Affairs initiative that converts a pedagogical goal into a graduation requirement. It carries enforcement authority, which Wave 1 documents typically lacked.

2
Glitch

Read the AI Fluency Initiative against OSU's Wave 1 corpus. In April 2023 Academic Affairs advised that AI tools “should not be used in the completion of course assignments unless an instructor” authorizes them. In November 2023 the trustees added “unauthorized use of generative artificial intelligence” to the Code of Student Conduct. The mandate never mentions either document. The conduct rule still stands beside it, unreconciled.

3
Reveal

The unacknowledged shift shows institutional positions on AI moving between waves. Wave 1 documents are now historical artifacts. Because OSU does not acknowledge the shift, its Wave 2 documents are structurally dishonest about what changed and why.

4
Abstraction

Institutional positions on AI shift from one wave to the next. When an institution leaves the transition from Wave 1 to Wave 2 unnarrated, the silence becomes a governance problem.

Three-Level Literacy Read

Conceptual

The initiative defines its goal in two parts: graduates will be “fluent in their field of study, and fluent in the application of AI in that field.” The definition ties AI fluency to disciplinary fluency. It says little about what fluency in AI itself includes, and the word carries assumptions of its own (see “fluency” under common terms).

Structural

The Office of Academic Affairs houses the mandate, and it holds the authority to require graduation outcomes. No document in the comparative set is better placed structurally.

Operative

A graduation requirement with no defined assessment framework leaves a wide operative gap. Instructors must develop AI fluency and have no shared standard for measuring it.

Discussion Notes

Read this entry in sequence with the OSU Wave 1 entries. Ask: what would an honest narrative of this policy shift look like? What would an institution need to say to its faculty, students, and the public when it adds a mandate on top of a prohibition it never repealed?
Students who submit work “drafted or edited in any way by an artificial intelligence (AI) content generator (including but not limited to OpenAI's ChatGPT, Microsoft's Bing AI Chatbot, and Google's Bard) without the prior and explicit approval by the instructor” face discipline. With approval, students disclose the tool and their prompts.

Four-Step Analysis

1
Genre

Student conduct policy update: a School of Law standards revision embedded in the professional conduct framework. The law school context places named tools under conduct rules, outside pedagogy.

2
Glitch

The rule covers any AI content generator, so its examples carry no legal weight. They still date it. Google renamed Bard in February 2024, and the standards as published in 2026 still name it. Here the temporal artifact problem becomes explicit.

3
Reveal

The named-tool policy shows Wave 1 governance calibrated to a specific technological moment, one already over. The policy now records what institutions feared in Spring 2023.

4
Abstraction

Tool-specific governance is structurally unsustainable. A policy that names tools and leaves capabilities undefined will need continuous revision, or it will go silently obsolete.

Three-Level Literacy Read

Conceptual

The named examples give students a concrete picture of what the rule covers. Each rename or new product makes the picture older.

Structural

The law school issued this document, and the university did not. Because the policy is specific to one discipline, it does not generalize. Other UCLA schools are free to adopt different frameworks.

Operative

The rule places the operative decision with each instructor and asks students for permission and disclosure. Disclosing prompts is a real operative demand. The rule still gives students no criteria for when AI use would serve the work.

Discussion Notes

The corpus offers no clearer case of the temporal artifact problem. Use it to discuss what happens when a policy's examples outlive the tools they name. Ask: what does a student read into a list of examples that includes a product which no longer exists?
"promotes the responsible use of artificial intelligence (“AI”) … to support and enhance its academic, research and administrative operations"

Four-Step Analysis

1
Genre

Formal institutional policy: a university-level document carrying legal force. UF has been building AI infrastructure since 2020. This document legitimizes the infrastructure after the fact.

2
Glitch

Since UF announced its $70 million NVIDIA partnership in July 2020, the university has built the HiPerGator AI supercomputer, hired more than 100 AI faculty, and opened an AI certificate to undergraduates in every major. The 2026 formal policy arrives after the infrastructure. It looks backward at decisions already in place.

3
Reveal

At UF, formal governance trailed institutional practice by years. The university built its AI infrastructure under informal arrangements. The formal policy then legitimized decisions already made. Governance here records choices made elsewhere.

4
Abstraction

Formal policy often looks backward. Across the corpus, institutions tend to formalize positions they have already put into practice. Their formal policy documents describe institutional practice and do little to shape it.

Three-Level Literacy Read

Conceptual

UF's formal policy organizes itself around "responsible use" and never defines responsible. The document assumes the definition and leaves the reader to supply it, a conceptual gap the text itself is unable to fill.

Structural

University administration houses the formal policy. Neither the AI initiative nor the faculty governance body owns it. This placement gives the policy legal authority and separates it from the pedagogical and research communities it purports to govern.

Operative

The document demands minimal operative literacy: use AI responsibly. The operative gap is total. It offers no decision framework, no assessment standard, and no mechanism for working out what responsible use looks like in specific contexts.

Discussion Notes

Use this entry to discuss how governance relates to practice. UF stands apart in the corpus for the scale and early date of its AI investment. Ask: what does it mean for a university to have a "responsible use" policy for a system it built, deployed, and requires students to use?

Part IV

Adaptable Policy Language

These templates offer starting language for AI use policies at three levels. Four principles from the corpus analysis shape them:

1. Scope terms functionally

Define AI by what it does (generates text, produces images, automates tasks). Product names date quickly, and named-tool policies go obsolete in months.

2. Date your policy

Every policy in this corpus became a historical artifact. Acknowledge the conditions of writing and commit to revision.

3. Name the revision trigger

Specify what would prompt an update: tool proliferation, new institutional guidance, or evidence of the policy producing the wrong outcomes.

4. Explain the reasoning

A policy students understand is easier for them to follow. State why the policy takes its position.

Treat the templates below as starting points to rewrite. Replace everything in [brackets]. Edit directly in each template, and this browser saves your changes automatically. Use the Print or save as PDF button to export your draft.

Course-Level: Syllabus Statement

Course · Syllabus
Bracket guidance: The first bracket asks for a functional definition, without product names. The second and third brackets ask you to decide for your course now. The attribution bracket asks you to name what disclosure looks like in practice. The final sentence commits you to revision, so build the time into your semester-end review.

Program-Level: Shared Governance Statement

Program · Handbook / Sequence Governance
Bracket guidance: The program statement leaves some questions open. Its job is to give individual instructors a framework to apply. The decisive move is to name the discipline-specific literacy the program values. That act of naming is the governance decision, and the rest of the statement follows from it.

Department-Level: Institutional Governance Framework

Department · Curriculum Committee / Faculty Governance
Bracket guidance: The department framework sets the conditions for course and program policy. Above all, it names who holds authority at each level and establishes a review mechanism. In the corpus, departments without such a framework tend to produce contradictory course-level policies. The contradictions arise because no one has named the shared commitments, even where instructors agree.

Part V

Submit to the Policy Repository

The repository grows in two ways. Participants submit their analyses and drafts, which show how faculty across disciplines read and write AI policy. Participants also add documents to the corpus, which the next collection sweep verifies and indexes. Submit either, or both. The form collects no name or email address. Submissions carry tags for discipline, institution type, and engagement only. Submissions go to Kirsti Cole at CWSP. Because they carry no name or email, CWSP cannot write back about them. To talk about your policy work, use the CWSP intake form.

Your analysis and drafts

Sends your Part III templates and Part IV drafts as they stand. Leave this unchecked to submit corpus documents alone.

Documents for the corpus

Add up to three AI governance documents from any institution. Each needs an institution, a title, and a link. The rest helps the index.

What is collected and how it is used. Submitting sends your templates, drafts, tags, and any documents you add to a CWSP archive. It collects no name, email address, or affiliation. CWSP uses submissions to track how faculty across disciplines read and write AI policy, to inform programming, and to grow the corpus. Documents you add are checked before they enter the index. Individual responses are not published in identifiable form. CWSP might use the archive as a research dataset; if the project proceeds under IRB review, consent procedures will be updated to match.
