Your path

    An adventure in three levels

    Policy decides for the student

    Policy decides for the student. That's the usual story, anyway. A syllabus clause permits AI here and forbids it there, and your part is to follow along. This book starts somewhere else, with the decisions you make about AI in your own work, one assignment at a time.

    The French theorist Michel de Certeau had a useful way to put this. Institutions set strategies, and people improvise tactics inside them. A policy is a strategy. What you do with a tool late at night, draft due in the morning and a syllabus with nothing to say on the matter, is a tactic, and it belongs to you. The anthropologist Brian Street noticed something close to this about literacy. Older accounts treated it as a skill a person has or lacks. Street saw it as a practice, something people do in particular places, for particular reasons, with something at stake.

    So this book asks for practice, which is a different thing from fluency. Fluency is ease with a tool, how fast and smoothly you get output from it. The framework behind this book calls what you'll exercise here operative literacy: judgment you're willing to stand behind, about when, how, and whether to use the tool, with your reasons attached.

    How the book works

    You'll move through three levels, in order. First you describe what the tool does. Then you look at how it's built and who it leaves at a disadvantage. Last, you decide what this situation calls for: use, limits, or refusal. Each passage ends with a choice and a passage to turn to. When a new tool or a new assignment comes along, you start again at the first level.

    Student decision

    No sentence yet

    No sentence yet? You're in good company. Plenty of the pack hits this spot, and it's a good place to stop for a minute.

    Often it happens like this. You asked for something broad, help with this paragraph, make it sound better, and the tool did several things at once. A word swapped here, a sentence turned around there, and by the end it's hard to say where its hand stopped and yours began. The framework has a name for this: treating AI as one thing. You asked for one thing, help, and the tool answered with several.

    Sometimes there's no use yet at all. The tool isn't open, and what you have is closer to a feeling, something like I need to get through this faster.

    And sometimes you know the sentence and would rather not write it, because putting it into words feels like the first step toward telling someone. Lots of people feel this, especially when the syllabus says nothing.

    Each of these has its own way forward.

    Untraced changes

    If you asked for broad help and lost track of what the tool changed, go back to the draft and ask where the thinking happened. Pick one sentence you're unsure about. What did the tool do to this sentence? Did it choose the word? The order? The claim? A use too blurry to name across a whole draft usually comes into focus at the size of one sentence, and one sentence is enough to answer the checkpoint.

    No use yet

    If what you have is a wish to move faster, start with the slow part. Which piece of the assignment drags for you? Writing an opening line, sorting out a source, turning notes into sentences? Name the piece. Wanting speed tells you nothing about what the tool would do to your thinking. Naming the task tells you what the tool would do, and what it would cost.

    Avoiding the name

    If you know the sentence and don't want to write it, take the reluctance seriously. It's information. Often it means you're unsure you'd defend the use, and better to learn so now, before the sentence goes into the draft.

    None of these counts against you. Refusal theory, the work this book draws on, treats declining to act, or declining to answer yet, as a position with standing of its own. Working out why the use won't name itself is the practice, at this scale.

    When you've figured out which of these fits, come back to the checkpoint and try the sentence again. If it comes, carry on. If it still won't come, you've learned something worth bringing to your instructor. A use without a name gives you nothing yet to defend, to yourself or to anyone else.

    Structural · Analyze

    How they are built, and whom they disadvantage.

    Key question: Who controls this system?

    Whose standard is the sentence meeting?

    You named it. Revising a paragraph, generating three openings, checking a citation format, whatever it was, you know where the tool's hand went and where yours did. Hold onto the sentence. The next question has nothing to do with whether the use was allowed. It asks whose standard the sentence meets once the tool has touched it.

    Here's what's going on underneath. The tool learned from an enormous pile of writing, and what it hands back reflects what was most common in the pile. Generative output leans toward the most statistically likely phrasing, and the most likely phrasing comes overwhelmingly from dominant, standardized English. Lean on the tool to draft or fix a sentence, and the sentence drifts toward its standard, away from your voice, your dialect, your way of getting to a point. If English isn't the language you think in first, the drift costs you more than it costs a classmate who already writes in the tool's default.

    Researchers have started to measure this. Moon and colleagues compared 372,793 college admissions essays written before and after ChatGPT came out (2025, a preprint). The later essays had bigger vocabularies and fewer ideas. The steepest losses in ideas fell on international applicants, Black applicants, and students from homes where English isn't the main language. Liang and colleagues found the other half of the pattern (2023). AI-writing detectors flag essays by writers whose first language isn't English as machine-written. The same writers get flattened by the generators and then suspected by the detectors. One structural condition produces both.

    So take a look at your sentence. How does it sound now, next to how it sounded before the tool touched it? Leave aside whether it got better. Does it still sound like you?

    There's a second cost, quieter than the first. The judgment behind a sentence, why this word, why this claim here and not three lines down, is usually what the assignment is there to build. Hand the sentence over, and the judgment goes with it. The tool has no ear for what sounds like you. You do, as long as your judgment stays in the sentence.

    Carry one question to the next checkpoint. If the judgment behind this sentence disappeared, would the assignment still be doing its job?

    Checkpoint two: the judgment test

    Is the judgment the tool supplies, in the use you named, the same judgment this assignment is built to develop?

    Three answers wait below. Yes and no are both findings, and either one takes you to the deciding level with something useful in hand. Uncertain is a fair answer too. It keeps you here a little longer, until a finding turns up.

    Your finding: no. Test it once.

    So the function you named leaves the assignment's real work with you. A citation formatted, a comma checked on a paper graded for its argument, a reference list put in order. Each of these changes the surface, and your claims, your order, and your reading of the sources stay where you put them.

    Before you move on, give the no one quick test. A narrow name at checkpoint one makes a no easy to reach, so compare what you asked for with what came back. If you asked for a grammar check and a sentence returned making a different claim, the tool did more than you named, and the answer has changed. Head back and answer again.

    If the no holds up, you have a finding, and it would be the same for any student using this function on this assignment. It tells you one thing about what comes next. The strongest reason to decline, a tool doing the thinking the assignment exists to build, doesn't apply here. Plenty is still open. The syllabus is as quiet as it was. You still get to choose a limit for the tool and decide what you'll disclose.

    Uncertain: stay here a little longer

    Uncertain usually means the test is hard to run, and it's an honest place to be. It tends to happen in one of three ways.

    You might not know what this assignment is built to develop. Courses rarely say so outright. The syllabus lists a topic, a length, a due date, a citation style, and leaves the skill underneath unnamed, whether it's building an argument, interpreting a text, or noticing what a source didn't already tell you. Without a name for the skill, the test has nothing to measure against.

    You might know the skill and still be unsure whether the tool crossed into it. Fixing grammar and reorganizing a paragraph around a claim you didn't choose are different acts, yet from inside the sentence the line between them is hard to see. Being unsure here is reasonable. This is what the line looks like up close.

    Or you might suspect the honest answer is yes, and uncertain feels easier to choose. Give this the same attention as the reluctance back at checkpoint one. Sit with it for a moment before settling it in whichever direction lets you keep going.

    For the first, look for the assignment's own account of itself: the prompt beyond the logistics, a line in the rubric, something your instructor said in class about what the paper is for. Put the skill into words. For the second, take your one sentence and sort what was corrected from what was decided. A misplaced comma is a correction. Putting a claim here instead of there is a decision. For the third, ask yourself plainly whether you already know the answer.

    Then reread the assignment with the skill in mind, and come back to the checkpoint. If the answer turns out to be yes, it's a finding like any other, and it brings you to the question at the next level: what this situation warrants.

    Operative · Decide

    When to use one, how, and when to refuse, with reasons tied to the task.

    Key question: What does this situation warrant: use, limits, or refusal?

    What will you decide, and on what grounds?

    You've named the use. You've worked out whether the tool's judgment is the judgment this assignment is built to develop, either straight away or after some time with the uncertainty. One question is left, and it's a different one from whether you're allowed to use the tool. It asks what you'll decide in this case, and why, now that you can see what's at stake.

    Here's a difference worth noticing. Your answer at checkpoint two would be the same for any student using this function on this assignment. The answer here belongs to you, and it exists only once you decide.

    This level asks what this situation warrants: use, limits, or refusal. All three are open to you. A decision to decline here covers this assignment and no other. It keeps the tool's habits of language from standing in for a voice this task is meant to grow, and you'll weigh it fresh whenever the conditions change.

    Three questions are worth answering wherever you land. Are you writing with the tool, or writing for it? Writing with it keeps your judgment at work in what you ask, what you keep, and what you revise. Writing for it hands your judgment to the output and arranges everything else around what came back. Have you asked your instructor? A silent policy leaves the question open. What will you disclose, and how? Disclosure protects you as much as it satisfies anyone else. Note what you used and how, the way you'd note a source.

    From here, most students land on one of three postures. None of them is the answer the book is hoping for. Each one holds up, as long as you have your reasons.

    Your decision: decline

    Position taken

    You've decided not to use the tool on this task. No rule made the call. You made it, because the judgment this assignment is built to develop is the very judgment the tool would supply in your place, whether it's a sentence-level choice, the shape of an argument, or your own reading of a text.

    This decision belongs to this task. Name what you're protecting, so the decision travels with its reasons. The person deciding owes their reasons, and here the person deciding is you, so let your instructor know. A refusal nobody hears about protects nothing, and it asks nothing of the person who set the assignment.

    This is refusal in the sense Simpson, Campt, and Benjamin describe: an accountable rejection of conditions which would otherwise reproduce harm, grounded in analysis instead of preference. Declining here, for this reason, on this task, is an answer you arrived at honestly.

    Your decision: use with conditions

    Position taken

    You've decided to use the tool, inside limits you set and state before you start. Limits written in advance are yours. Limits written afterward to match what already happened are an explanation attached after the fact.

    Three things make a conditional use conditional. Name the specific function, outlining, checking grammar, generating alternatives, in terms narrow enough to mean something. Set a limit on what the tool is allowed to touch (a first pass, say, and leave the final draft to you) and hold to it. Note what you used and how, with the care you'd give a citation, so a reader knows where it came from.

    The limit is what makes the choice yours. Set it before you open the tool.

    Your decision: defer

    Position taken

    Something the decision depends on is still unknown, the policy, the assignment's purpose, or what the tool would do to it. You've decided to find out before you write another sentence.

    A silent policy leaves the question open, and the deadline pressing on you is no reason to answer it by default. The framework has a name for deciding by default: a decision made by the clock in place of the person. So ask what the assignment is trying to develop, and if the syllabus doesn't say, ask your instructor directly.

    A deadline changes how soon you need the answer. The question still deserves one. Deferring is a choice, and it keeps a due date from making the choice for you.
