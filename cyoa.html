Your path

    Policy decides for the student

    Policy decides for the student. A syllabus clause permits AI in one place and forbids it in another, and the student's part is to comply. This tool begins with the decisions you make about AI in your own work, one assignment at a time.

    The French theorist Michel de Certeau distinguished the strategies institutions set from the tactics people improvise inside them. A policy is a strategy. What you do with a tool late at night, with a draft due and a syllabus silent on the question, is a tactic, and it belongs to you. The anthropologist Brian Street drew a related distinction. Earlier accounts treated literacy as a fixed skill a person either has or lacks. Street described it as a practice, carried out in particular situations with particular stakes.

    The practice asked of you here is distinct from fluency. Fluency names ease with a tool, how quickly and smoothly you get output from it. Operative literacy names accountable judgment, a decision about when, how, and whether to use the tool, with reasons you will stand behind.

    Three levels, in order

    Practice is the word for what you exercise here. You will move through three levels in order. First you describe what the tool does. Then you analyze how it is built and whom it disadvantages. Last you decide what this situation warrants: use, limits, or refusal. Every new tool or assignment starts the sequence again at the first level.

    The situation

    Student decision

    You are working on a writing assignment. The syllabus does not mention AI. You are not sure whether the silence means permission, or means your instructor has not gotten to the question yet.

    What the systems are, and how they produce text.

    The use will not name itself

    Perhaps you cannot. Worth sitting with, rather than pushing past.

    Not being able to name the use usually means one of a few things. You asked the tool for something broad, help with this paragraph, make this sound better, and the tool did more than you tracked, restructuring a sentence here, replacing a word there, until you could not point to where its hand ended and yours began. The framework names this as a sign the describing level is missing, treating AI as one thing. Your request named one thing, help. The tool answered it with several functions. Or you have not opened the tool yet, and the plan in your head is not a use at all, it is a feeling, something like get through this faster, with no particular function attached. Or you can name it, and are avoiding saying so, because naming it out loud, even to yourself, feels like the first step toward disclosing it, and disclosure is the part you have been avoiding.

    Each of these is a different problem, and each calls for a different next move.

    Untraced changes

    If you asked for broad help and can no longer tell which changes came from the tool, return to the draft itself and ask where thinking happened. Find one sentence you are unsure about. Ask what the tool did to the sentence specifically, not the paragraph, not the assignment, the sentence. Did it choose the word. Did it choose the order. Did it choose the claim. A use you cannot name at the scale of the whole draft, you can usually still name at the scale of one sentence, and naming it there is enough to answer the checkpoint honestly.

    No use yet

    If there is no use yet, only a feeling of wanting to move faster, the feeling is not nothing, but it is not a plan either. Ask which part of the assignment is slow for you, generating an opening line, organizing a source, turning research notes into sentences, and name the part. Speed is not a function. Wanting to draft faster tells you nothing about what the tool would be doing to your thinking. Naming the function tells you both what it does and what it costs.

    Avoiding the name

    If you can name it and are avoiding saying so because naming feels like confession, the discomfort is information, not an obstacle to get past. An unclear policy is not a dare. The discomfort is a sign the use is not one you are certain you can defend, and worth knowing before the sentence goes into the draft rather than after.

    None of these three moves is a penalty for reaching this checkpoint with no answer. Refusal theory treats declining to act, or declining to answer yet, as a position with its own standing, not a failure to comply. Sitting here, working out why the use will not name itself, is already the practice this checkpoint asks you to build. It is not a detour on the way to the real work. It is the real work, at this scale.

    When you have worked through which of these you are facing, return to the checkpoint. Try naming the use again, in one sentence. If you can now, continue. If you still cannot, this is an answer too, and one worth bringing to your instructor directly. A use without a name gives you nothing yet to defend, to yourself or to anyone else.

    Structural · Analyze

    How they are built, and whom they disadvantage.

    Key question: Who controls this system?

    Whose standard the sentence meets

    You named it. Whatever the sentence was, revising a paragraph for clarity, generating three possible openings, checking a citation format, you can point to where the tool's hand went and where yours went. Hold onto the sentence. The next question does not test whether the use was permitted. It tests whose standard the sentence is meeting once the tool has touched it.

    The tool was trained on an enormous body of writing, and the sentence it produced, or the revision it offered, reflects what was most common in it, not what is most true to you. Generative output favors the most statistically likely phrasing, and the most likely phrasing is drawn overwhelmingly from dominant, standardized English. Lean on the tool to draft or fix a sentence, and the sentence drifts toward the tool's standard, away from your own voice, your own dialect, your own way of getting to a point. If English is not the language you think in first, this cost lands on you more heavily than on a classmate for whom the standard the tool defaults to is already home.

    The evidence for this drift is recent. Moon and colleagues compared 372,793 admissions essays written before and after the release of ChatGPT (2025, preprint). The later essays gained vocabulary and lost ideas. The steepest losses in ideas fell on international applicants, Black applicants, and students from non-English-speaking households. A second study completes the pattern. Liang and colleagues found AI-writing detectors misclassify essays by writers whose first language is not English as machine-generated (2023). Generators flatten these writers first, and detectors then suspect them. One structural condition produces both.

    Notice what the sentence sounds like now compared to what it sounded like before the tool touched it. Not whether it improved, whether it still sounds like you.

    There is a second cost, quieter than the first. The judgment behind a sentence, why this word and not another, why this claim goes here and not three lines down, is usually the thing the assignment exists to develop and grade. Hand a sentence to the tool, and the judgment behind it goes with it. The tool has no sense of what sounds like you. Only you have it, and only if your own judgment stays in the sentence rather than the tool's.

    Ask, of the sentence you are holding: if the judgment behind this sentence disappeared, would the assignment still be doing its job. If the answer is no, the sentence has moved from an act of writing to an act of accepting output, and this is the finding checkpoint two asks you to sit with.

    Checkpoint two: the judgment test

    Is the judgment the tool supplies, in the use you named, the same judgment this assignment is built to develop?

    There are three buttons here, yes, no, and uncertain. Yes and no are both findings, and each carries you to the operative level with different information. Uncertain is a position in its own right. It keeps you at this level until a finding comes.

    Your finding: no. Test it once.

    The function you named leaves the assignment's judgment with you. A citation formatted, a comma checked on a paper graded for its argument, a reference list alphabetized, each of these changes the surface of the work and leaves its claims, its order, and its reading of the sources where you put them.

    Before you move on, test the no once. A no comes easily when the use was named narrowly at checkpoint one, so check what the tool did against what you asked of it. If you asked for a grammar check and a sentence came back making a different claim, the tool did more than the function you named, and the answer here has changed. Return to the checkpoint and answer again.

    If the no holds, it is a finding, and it would be the same for any student using this function on this assignment. It tells you one thing about the decision ahead. The reason for declining named at the operative level, a tool supplying the judgment the assignment exists to develop, is absent here. The decision is still open. The syllabus is as silent as it was when you started. You have yet to choose a limit for the tool, and you have yet to say what you will disclose.

    Uncertain: stay at this level until a finding comes

    Uncertain here usually means the test itself is hard to run, not avoidance. A few ways this happens.

    You may not know, with any precision, what judgment this specific assignment is built to develop. Courses rarely say it in those terms. The syllabus names a topic, a length, a due date, a citation style, and leaves the actual capacity under all of it unstated, whether it is argument construction, or original interpretation of a text, or the ability to notice something a source did not already say for you. Without naming the capacity, the test, would the assignment still be doing its job without this sentence's judgment, has nothing to check against.

    You may know the capacity in general and still be unsure whether what the tool did crossed into it. A grammar check and a paragraph reorganized around a claim you did not choose are not the same act, and the line between assisting a mechanical task and absorbing an intellectual one is not always visible from inside the sentence. Reasonable uncertainty here is not evasion. It is what the line looks like up close.

    You may also suspect the honest answer is yes, and uncertain is where the suspicion goes to avoid becoming a finding. This is close to the discomfort checkpoint one already asked you to notice, and it deserves the same treatment, sit with it rather than resolve it by default toward whichever answer lets you keep going.

    For the first case, find the assignment's own account of itself, the prompt language beyond the logistics, a rubric line, a comment your instructor made in class about what the paper is for, and put words to the capacity being developed. For the second, take the one sentence again and separate what was corrected from what was decided. A misplaced comma is correction. A claim placed here rather than there is a decision. For the third, ask directly, do I already know the answer and am reluctant to write it down.

    Reread the assignment with the capacity you named in hand, then return to this checkpoint. An answer of yes here is not a violation to hide. It brings you to the operative question, what this situation warrants, and this question is next.

    Operative · Decide

    When to use one, how, and when to refuse, with reasons tied to the task.

    Key question: What does this situation warrant: use, limits, or refusal?

    What will you decide, and on what grounds?

    You have named the use. You have worked out whether the judgment the tool supplies is the judgment this assignment is built to develop, either directly or by sitting with the uncertainty until an answer came. Whichever way you arrived here, one question is left, and it is not whether you are allowed to use the tool. It is what you will decide in this particular case, and on what grounds, now that you can see what is at stake. Your answer at checkpoint two would be the same for any student using this function on this assignment. The answer here belongs to you alone, and it exists only once you decide.

    This is the operative level, and its central question is what this situation warrants: use, limits, or refusal. Declining the tool here is not a blanket position against it, in this assignment or any other. It is a decision not to let the norms embedded in the tool's training stand in for a voice this task exists to develop. Refusal at this level is not a rule you carry into every assignment. It is a judgment you make about this one, worth remaking each time the conditions change.

    Three questions are yours to answer wherever you land. Are you writing with the tool or writing for it. Writing with the tool keeps your own judgment active at every stage, in what you ask it, what you keep, what you revise. Writing for the tool hands your judgment to its output and adjusts everything else around what it gave you. Have you asked your instructor. A policy's silence is not permission, it is a question still open. What will you disclose, and how. Disclosure protects you as much as it satisfies a rule. Note what you used and how, the way you would note a source.

    From here, most students land on one of three postures. None is the correct answer waiting to be found. Each is a defensible position, provided you can say why.

    Operative · Decide

    When to use one, how, and when to refuse, with reasons tied to the task.

    Your decision: decline

    Position taken

    You decide not to use the tool on this task, not because a rule forbids it, but because the judgment this assignment is built to develop is the exact judgment the tool would supply in your place. A sentence-level choice, the structure of an argument, your own reading of a text, whichever it is, using the tool here would let the tool's training answer where the assignment asks you to.

    Declining is not a blanket stance you carry to the next assignment. Name, specifically, what you are protecting, so the decision travels with reasons instead of habit. Apply it to this task, not to every task the syllabus contains. The person deciding owes their reasons, and here the person deciding is you. Say so to your instructor rather than deciding privately and hoping the choice goes unnoticed. A refusal nobody knows about protects nothing, and asks nothing of the person who assigned the work.

    This is what refusal theory names at the scale of one assignment. Simpson, Campt, and Benjamin describe refusal not as noncompliance but as an accountable rejection of conditions which would otherwise reproduce harm, grounded in analysis rather than preference. Declining the tool here, for this reason, on this task, is refusal in exactly this sense. It is not the absence of an answer. It is the answer, arrived at honestly.

    Your decision: use with conditions

    Position taken

    You decide to use the tool, but only inside limits you set and state before you start, not limits you discover afterward and describe as though they were always there.

    Three things have to be explicit for a conditional use to be conditional at all. Name the specific function, outlining, checking grammar, generating alternatives, rather than describing the use in general terms broad enough to cover almost anything. Set a limit on what the tool is allowed to touch, a first pass rather than a final draft, and hold to it. Note what you used and how, treated with the same care you would give a citation, since a source the reader cannot trace is not disclosed, only mentioned.

    Conditional use without a stated limit is not conditional use. It is unexamined use with an explanation attached after the fact, and the difference matters more than it looks like it should. The limit is what makes the choice yours rather than the tool's, and a limit set before you open the tool is a different act than a justification written afterward to match what already happened.

    Your decision: defer

    Position taken

    Something the decision depends on is still unknown, the policy, the assignment's purpose, or what the tool would do to it, and rather than guessing, you decide to find out before you write another sentence.

    An unclear policy is not permission, and it is not a loophole either. It is an open question, and the deadline pressing on you is not a reason to answer it by default. Deciding by default is the sign the framework gives for a missing operative level, a decision made by the clock in place of the person. Ask what the assignment is trying to develop before assuming you already know. Ask your instructor directly if the syllabus does not say, rather than reading its silence as a decision already made in your favor.

    A deadline changes how soon the answer is needed. It does not change whether the question deserves one. Deferring here is not indecision. It is choosing not to let a due date stand in for a choice still yours to make.
