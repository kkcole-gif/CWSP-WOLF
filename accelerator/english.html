English: AI Literacy and Policy Workbook

This workbook is for the English working group and its department head, including members who have not worked on learning outcomes or AI policy before. Working through it, the group decides what the program does about AI for each of its learning outcomes, and CWSP has already filled in the unit's record and the policy research.

Your project

Two English proposals use this workbook. The AI Learning Pathway, from Huiling Ding and Jason Swarts, plans a 12-credit graduate certificate in Generative AI for Content Development and Writing, building on the OASIS project. The Professional Writing project, from Christin Phelps and Elizabeth Wagner, builds a framework of AI literacies for the ENG 33x courses. The questions and outcomes below are the pathway's.

How this works

Every program files learning outcomes with the university, short statements of what its students learn. In this workbook the working group takes those outcomes one at a time and answers four questions about each:

  1. Where do students show this outcome? (the course and the assignment)
  2. What do students produce or perform there? (a paper, a talk, a lab report, a poster)
  3. What does an AI tool do with this kind of work?
  4. What should the program decide about AI for it, and why?

Many outcomes name a kind of thinking, such as "analyze" or "explain," without saying what students write or present to show it. When an outcome says only "analyze," a grader has nothing specific to check, so a student's analysis and one an AI tool generated look much the same against the outcome. The second question supplies what such an outcome leaves out, which is why the questions come in this order.

The five parts, and when to do them

  1. Part 1, Literacy. Read CWSP's short account of AI in your discipline and answer three questions. About 20 minutes, before the first session.
  2. Part 2, Baseline. Look over the outcomes your programs filed and CWSP's notes on them. About 10 minutes, before the first session.
  3. Part 3, Map. Try one of your own assignments in an AI tool, then answer the first three questions for each outcome in a session. Ten minutes before January, then the January session.
  4. Part 4, Policy. Read a short summary of what other departments and the field's organizations have decided. About 55 minutes, before the first session.
  5. Part 5, Lab. Answer the fourth question for each outcome, give the reason, and write up the decision. The Lab sessions, February and March.

CWSP and your department head set the session dates. To submit, the group needs answers for at least one outcome in Part 3.

One recorder, one copy

Because the workbook saves in one web browser at a time, the department head names one recorder (often the head), who keeps the unit's copy and types for the group during and between sessions. Everyone else reads the same pages. Members who want to jot down answers before a session do so in their own browser, where no one else sees them until they bring them to the session. After each session the recorder downloads the rows at the end of the page and pastes them into the unit's Google Sheet (opens in a new window), which holds what the group agreed.

Part 1 · LiteracyThree levels, in English

About 20 minutes, before the first session.

CWSP's framework reads AI at three levels, taken in order: describe what the system is and what it does to an act, analyze whose interests and labor it carries and what it leaves out, and decide what the program does about it. Above each prompt is CWSP's reading of the level in your discipline, which the working group corrects wherever it misdescribes the field.

Describe

English studies the system's medium. A large language model produces text by predicting likely sequences from a corpus of prior writing, and the department's programs name the acts the model imitates: interpreting texts under a framework (BA, MA), making a work of original accomplishment (MFA), analyzing what writing technologies do to text, its invention, and its use (Technical Communication MS, outcome 2), and framing questions in a conceptual vocabulary (CRDM). Describing the system in the unit's terms means naming, program by program, which of these acts a model performs on the surface.

List what students write, say, build, or present in the program, such as a seminar paper, a poster, a lab report, or a capstone talk. These acts return in Part 3, one per outcome.

Analyze

The structural questions belong to the field's own traditions: authorship and voice, the labor and the uncredited texts in a training corpus, whose language a model treats as standard, and what a generated reading or draft leaves out. The Technical Communication MS already states this analysis as an outcome. The CRDM PhD names ethical practice, including labor, in outcome 1d. Each program brings a different body of theory to the same question.

A sentence or two is enough. The exercise at the start of Part 3 gives the group evidence for this answer.

Decide

The operative question differs by program, because a literary interpretation, a poem, a set of instructions for a user, and a dissertation chapter certify different judgments, so the department's decisions will differ by program and by assignment. For each outcome the Lab records the decision and its reason, counting refusal with a reason as a decision.

Name two or three, which the Lab returns to. See Terms used here for what the phrase means.

How the codebook counts

CWSP counts an outcome as documenting communication when the outcome, or the objective above it, names explicit communication (write, present, audience, rhetoric) or visual and multimodal work (visual, media, design). Genres (thesis, report), curricular sites (capstone, conference), and cognitive verbs (analyze, explain) are recorded and never counted, since each names a product, a place, or a kind of thinking without the act in which a student communicates it.

Part 4 · PolicyWhat peers and the field say

About 55 minutes, before the first session.

These readings let the working group see what other departments and the field's societies have written, and where their wording created problems for the people following it, so the unit's own statement avoids the same problems.

In brief. Most peer English departments leave AI rules to each instructor, with Georgia Tech's writing program the exception, since it requires every instructor to hold a written policy. In March 2026 the field's society for college writing teachers (CCCC) affirmed a right of students and teachers to refuse AI, which any program policy at NC State will need to respect.

The full finding and each document (14)

CWSP read the unit-level record in English and writing programs at NC State's twelve official peers, then the statements of the field's societies: MLA, CCCC, AWP for creative writing, the IEEE Professional Communication Society for technical communication, and AWAC for writing across the curriculum. For each document, the entry gives its scope and CWSP's reading of where its language breaks down, offered for the working group to correct. For the AI Learning Pathway, CWSP added three certificates in AI and writing at other universities, two graduate and one undergraduate, read for their structure and for where ethics enters the requirements.

Peer English departments have written statements of position and left rules to instructors. Rutgers English argues for critical AI literacy and leaves the decision to each instructor. Texas A&M English offers instructors two syllabus paragraphs, documented use or prohibition, under a preamble standing against AI as a replacement for writing, the Georgia writing program declines a single policy, and Georgia Tech's writing program requires every instructor to hold one, chosen from four options. Across the same years the field moved from guidance to rights: the MLA-CCCC task force recommended policy set in tiers (institution, program, class) in 2024, and in March 2026 CCCC affirmed a right of students and teachers to refuse generative AI in the writing classroom. A program writing policy at NC State would decide at the level the 2024 guidance asks for while protecting the refusal the 2026 resolution affirms. Because each of the department's programs also answers to a different society, the MFA, the Technical Communication MS, and the literature and rhetoric programs face different disciplinary statements.

How to read a policy for where its language breaks

When authors write governance documents under institutional pressure, the language records more than they intended. Five principles for durable policy language
  1. Scope terms by function. Define AI by what it does (generates text, produces images, automates tasks), since product names date within a term.
  2. Date the policy. State the term the policy covers and commit to revision.
  3. Name the revision trigger. Say what would prompt an update: new tools, new institutional guidance, or evidence of the policy producing the wrong outcomes.
  4. Separate principle from prohibition. State the commitment (what the program develops and assesses) apart from the rule (which functions are barred this term), because the commitment outlasts any one term's rule.
  5. Give the reason. A policy with its reason is easier to follow, and a later reviewer knows what to keep when the tools change.

Revised outcome

For an outcome naming only the thinking. Name the act, the object, the audience, and the student's accountability for AI decisions.
