Psychology: AI Literacy and Policy Workbook

This workbook is for the Psychology working group and its chair, including members who have not worked on learning outcomes or AI policy before. Working through it, the group moves from the unit's filed learning outcomes to written decisions about AI, one outcome at a time, and CWSP has already filled in the unit's record and the policy research.

What your unit produces

By the end of March the working group will have a decision and a reason for each of the unit's learning outcomes, and each decision becomes a short written product. CWSP prepared one finished example from Psychology as an illustration, which the group's own work replaces:

One finished outcome, as an illustration
The outcome as the program filed it2. Review the literature related to their research questions, demonstrating a comprehensive understanding of and a critical perspective toward the literature.
The decisionLimit
The reasonThe critical perspective on the literature is the judgment the degree certifies. Students are permitted to use a model to locate and sort studies. They read the studies they cite, write the synthesis and the critique themselves, verify every citation, and state in the proposal what the tool supplied.
The revised outcomeStudents will write a review of the literature related to their research questions which synthesizes and critiques prior studies they have read and verified, and will state the reasons for their decisions about any AI use in producing it.

Besides revised outcomes, the products include an AI policy for a single assignment, a rubric criterion for grading how students reason about AI, and a one-page statement of the program's position. Part 5 gives a fill-in frame for each.

How this works

Each learning outcome a program files with the university says what its students learn. Many outcomes name a kind of thinking (analyze, explain) without naming what students write, say, or make to show it, and when an outcome names only the thinking, a faculty member reading student work has no stated act to check, so a model's output and a student's work look alike against it. For this reason the questions run in a fixed order: where students show the outcome, what they do there, what a model now does to the act, and what the program decides.

  1. Literacy. Read the three levels (describe, analyze, decide) in your discipline's terms. About 20 minutes, before the first session.
  2. Baseline. Check CWSP's reading of your filed outcomes. About 10 minutes, before the first session.
  3. Map. Try one of your own assignments in an AI tool, then name for each outcome the course, the assessor, the act, and what a model does to it. Ten minutes before January, then the January session.
  4. Policy. Read what peer departments and the field's societies have written. About 30 minutes, before the first session.
  5. Lab. Decide, give the reason, and write the product. The Lab sessions, February and March.

CWSP and your chair set the session dates. To submit, the group needs at least one outcome mapped in Part 3.

One scribe, one draft

Because the workbook saves in one browser at a time, the chair names one scribe (often the chair), who holds the unit's draft and types for the group during and between sessions. Other members read the same pages, and any member who wants to draft answers before a session types them in their own browser, where they stay private until the member brings them to the session. After each session the scribe downloads the worksheet rows at the end of the page and pastes them into the unit's Google Sheet worksheet (opens in a new window), which holds what the group agreed.

How saving works

Everything typed here stays in this browser until someone clears it. To continue on another computer, or to hand the draft to the next scribe, select Save and email me a link, and whoever opens the link sees the draft as it was left. When the group finishes, Submit sends the workbook to CWSP and emails the scribe a copy.

Part 1 · LiteracyThree levels, in Psychology

About 20 minutes, before the first session.

CWSP's framework reads AI at three levels, taken in order: describe what the system is and what it does to an act, analyze whose interests and labor it carries and what it leaves out, and decide what the program does about it. Above each prompt is CWSP's reading of the level in your discipline, which the working group corrects wherever it misdescribes the field.

Describe

Psychology studies the cognition a model imitates and runs the empirical method a model now enters at every stage. A large language model predicts likely text from a corpus. In the department's outcomes, the acts it performs on the surface are the graduate research acts (proposing a question, reviewing the literature, planning collection and analysis, critiquing a study) and, for the BA, explaining scientific results to an audience. Describing the system in the unit's terms means naming which of these acts a model performs and which it only appears to perform.

List what students write, say, build, or present in the program, such as a seminar paper, a poster, a lab report, or a capstone talk. These acts return in Part 3, one per outcome.

Analyze

The structural questions come from the discipline's own commitments: research ethics and participant confidentiality (what happens to data entered into a tool), the validity of a generated citation or summary, bias in systems trained on unrepresentative text, and the APA's disclosure rules for authors. For students in school psychology and other applied concentrations, the same questions reach clients and schools through the APA's 2025 ethical guidance for practice and the guidance of NASP.

A sentence or two is enough. The exercise at the start of Part 3 gives the group evidence for this answer.

Decide

The operative question is which acts are the judgment the degree certifies. CWSP's baseline reading places the judgment in BA outcome 1 and in graduate outcomes 1, 2, 3, and 5, none of which names where a student performs it. For each outcome the Lab records the decision and its reason, counting refusal with a reason as a decision.

Name two or three, which the Lab returns to. See Terms used here for what the phrase means.

Part 2 · BaselineYour record

About 10 minutes, before the first session.

CWSP read every learning outcome the unit's programs filed with the university (SLO_Coded_2026, the coded export of the university assessment system, read under the CWSP Codebook 2026). Psychology BA, MS, and PhD. The MS and PhD file the same six outcomes, so the graduate rows appear once, and the PhD concentrations (among them school psychology) file no separate outcomes. Because the codebook counts what the filed outcomes document, silence in the record shows what the outcomes leave unwritten and nothing about what the courses teach.

How the codebook counts

CWSP counts an outcome as documenting communication when the outcome, or the objective above it, names explicit communication (write, present, audience, rhetoric) or visual and multimodal work (visual, media, design). Genres (thesis, report), curricular sites (capstone, conference), and cognitive verbs (analyze, explain) are recorded and never counted, since each names a product, a place, or a kind of thinking without the act in which a student communicates it.

CWSP's reading

Psychology BA. Documented on one of five outcomes. Outcome 2 names the act (explaining and describing scientific results to an audience in professional style) and is the only outcome naming a piece of writing or a presentation in which a student shows it. Outcome 1 holds the judgment the degree certifies (critical and creative thinking, skeptical inquiry, the scientific approach) without naming where a student performs it, so the outcome a model most affects and the outcome the record documents are different outcomes. Outcomes 3 through 5 name knowledge, professional readiness, and ethical standards.

Psychology MS and PhD. Documented on one of six outcomes. The MS and PhD file the same six statements. Outcome 6 names professional communication in the genre with its style guidelines, which in psychology means APA style and the empirical report. Outcomes 1 through 5 name research acts (propose questions, review the literature, plan collection and analysis, apply a critical perspective to one's own and others' research) without a communicative code, and these are the acts a current model performs most readily, the literature review above all. Outcome 2 is where the Lab's question is sharpest for this program. Documented on one of six outcomes for the PhD, on the same record. For the doctorate the question of which outcomes carry the degree's judgment turns on outcomes 1, 3, and 5, since the dissertation is the site where a student proposes, plans, and critiques, and the record names none of those sites.

Part 4 · PolicyWhat peers and the field say

About 30 minutes, before the first session.

These readings let the working group see what other departments and the field's societies have written, and where their wording created problems for the people following it, so the unit's own statement avoids the same problems.

In brief. Psychology's clearest rule comes from the APA and covers authors of journal articles: AI use is allowed if the author discloses it and checks everything the tool supplies. Two peer departments (Maryland and Georgia) apply the rule to graduate students, and no peer department has written guidance for undergraduates.

The full finding and each document (9)

CWSP read the unit-level record in psychology departments at NC State's twelve official peers, then the American Psychological Association and its divisions, the National Association of School Psychologists, and the nearest department policies outside the peer set. For each document, the entry gives its scope and CWSP's reading of where its language breaks down, offered for the working group to correct.

Of the three Accelerator units, psychology has the most developed disciplinary rule, and it is a rule for authors. The APA permits authors in its journals to use AI with disclosure in the methods section, requires them to verify what a tool supplies, and bars editors and reviewers from entering manuscripts into a model. Peer departments have carried the publication rule over to students, and none has written a rule for learning. Maryland and Georgia both send graduate students to the APA policy, both require disclosure, and both leave the decision to advisers, chairs, and instructors, with one fixed point at Maryland, where first drafts of theses and dissertations are written without AI. Outside the peer set, Buffalo forbids AI for text, outlines, presentation scripts, and statistical analysis in graduate milestones. Since the undergraduate program has the least guidance anywhere in the record, NC State Psychology would decide whether the BA follows the author's rule of disclosure or a learner's rule about which acts a student performs unaided.

University of Maryland

Department of Psychology · NC State peer, land-grant

Document
INTERIM Policy on Generative/Agentic AI Usage for Graduate Students (opens in a new window), Last updated April 29, 2026
Governs
Graduate coursework, research, TA work, comprehensive exams, theses and dissertations
What it says
Seven general rules: discuss AI at the start of every project, disclose AI-generated text, code, materials, images, and analysis (tool, version, prompts, and output), protect others' privacy, check every output, write first drafts of theses and dissertations without AI, let program areas set exam rules, and follow each supervisor's added rules. Checklists for each context leave the permitted and prohibited columns for supervisors to fill. Grammar checkers are not counted as generative AI.
Where the language breaks
Where the department directs clinical students to the clinic's AI policy, the words INSERT HERE stand in for the reference, so the students who work with clients, where confidentiality matters most, receive no location for the policy they are told to follow. Because the department left every checklist blank for supervisors to fill, the document sets a procedure for deciding and leaves each decision to a supervisor.

How to read a policy for where its language breaks

When authors write governance documents under institutional pressure, the language records more than they intended. Look for hedging (strongly discouraged), contradictions between sections or between two documents on one campus, silences where governance should be explicit, frameworks borrowed beyond their scope, and dates (language accurate when written and no longer true). The full method and the AI Governance Census are in the AI Policy Workbook (opens in a new window).

Part 5 · LabDecisions

The Lab sessions, February and March.

The program chosen under Who you are applies here as well.

Each outcome ends with a decision, the reason in the discipline's words, and the product the decision becomes. The Lab counts refusal with a reason as a decision, and the act mapped in Part 3 appears above each decision for reference.

Use
Students are permitted to use AI for the act, and they disclose what the tool did.
Limit
Students are permitted to use AI for named parts of the act, do the rest themselves, and state what the tool supplied.
Refuse
Students do the act without AI, and the program states its reason.

Products

Each decision becomes one of four products. Insert a frame, then rewrite it in your program's words.

Five principles for durable policy language
  1. Scope terms by function. Define AI by what it does (generates text, produces images, automates tasks), since product names date within a term.
  2. Date the policy. State the term the policy covers and commit to revision.
  3. Name the revision trigger. Say what would prompt an update: new tools, new institutional guidance, or evidence of the policy producing the wrong outcomes.
  4. Separate principle from prohibition. State the commitment (what the program develops and assesses) apart from the rule (which functions are barred this term), because the commitment outlasts any one term's rule.
  5. Give the reason. A policy with its reason is easier to follow, and a later reviewer knows what to keep when the tools change.

Revised outcome

For an outcome naming only the thinking. Name the act, the object, the audience, and the student's accountability for AI decisions.

Assignment-level AI policy

Write one policy for each assignment, with its reason, since a general syllabus clause gives students no reason tied to the task.

Rubric criterion for reasoning about use

The criterion grades the student's reasoning about use, so a student who refuses AI and gives reasons meets it.

Program philosophy of use

One page recording the program's decisions and their reasons, kept as the Lab's written record.
