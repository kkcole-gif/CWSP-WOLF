This workbook is for the STS working group and its department head, including members who have not worked on learning outcomes or AI policy before.   4. What would a flagship module or special topics course housed in STS look like?
  5. Is there demand, and a workable structure, for a graduate course on critical thinking and the ethical use of AI, as the seed of an STS graduate certificate?

How this works

Every program files learning outcomes with the university, short statements of what its students learn. In this workbook the working group takes those outcomes one at a time and answers four questions about each:

  1. Where do students show this outcome? (the course and the assignment)
  2. What do students produce or perform there? (a paper, a talk, a lab report, a poster)
  3. What does an AI tool do with this kind of work?
  4. What should the program decide about AI for it, and why?

Many outcomes name a kind of thinking, such as "analyze" or "explain," without saying what students write or present to show it. When an outcome says only "analyze," a grader has nothing specific to check, so a student's analysis and one an AI tool generated look much the same against the outcome. The second question supplies what such an outcome leaves out, which is why the questions come in this order.

The five parts, and when to do them

  1. Part 1, Literacy. Read CWSP's short account of AI in your discipline and answer three questions. Before the first session in January.
  2. Part 2, Baseline. Look over the outcomes your programs filed and CWSP's notes on them. Before the first session in January.
  3. Part 3, Map. Try one of your own assignments in an AI tool, then answer the first three questions for each outcome in a session. January through March, alongside the syllabus review and the labor-market analysis.
  4. Part 4, Policy. Read a short summary of what other departments and the field's organizations have decided. About 30 minutes, during the peer comparison, February through April.
  5. Part 5, Lab. Answer the fourth question for each outcome, give the reason, and write up the decision. May 15 through June 30, alongside the course design.

CWSP and your department head set the session dates. To submit, the group needs answers for at least one outcome in Part 3.

One recorder, one copy

Because the workbook saves in one web browser at a time, the department head names one recorder (often the head), who keeps the unit's copy and types for the group during and between sessions. Everyone else reads the same pages. Members who want to jot down answers before a session do so in their own browser, where no one else sees them until they bring them to the session. After each session the recorder downloads the rows at the end of the page and pastes them into the unit's Google Sheet (opens in a new window), which holds what the group agreed.

Who you are

Part 1 · LiteracyThree levels, in STS

Before the first session in January.

CWSP's framework reads AI at three levels, taken in order: describe what the system is and what it does to an act, analyze whose interests and labor it carries and what it leaves out, and decide what the program does about it. Above each prompt is CWSP's reading of the level in your discipline, which the working group corrects wherever it misdescribes the field.

Describe

In STS the system under description is also the object of study. A large language model predicts likely text from a corpus assembled by particular firms, trained with particular labor, and run on particular infrastructure. Describing it in the unit's own terms means naming the acts the outcomes ask of students (tracing a technology across accounts, explaining a concept, applying a method) and saying which of those acts a model performs on the surface.

List what students write, say, build, or present in the program, such as a seminar paper, a poster, a lab report, or a capstone talk. These acts return in Part 3, one per outcome.

Analyze

The structural question is the field's home ground: whose data, labor, energy, and interests the tool carries into a student's analysis, and what a generated synthesis leaves out of an account of a sociotechnical system. The proposed STS 214 unit on AI's material and systemic dimensions places this analysis in the curriculum as content. The Accelerator asks the unit to apply the same analysis to its own assignments.

A sentence or two is enough. The exercise at the start of Part 3 gives the group evidence for this answer.

Decide

The operative question is which acts are the judgment the STS degree certifies, and what the program says about a tool supplying the surface of those acts. For each outcome the Lab records the decision and the reason the program gives for it, counting refusal with a reason as a decision.

Name two or three, which the Lab returns to. See Terms used here for what the phrase means.

How the codebook counts

CWSP counts an outcome as documenting communication when the outcome, or the objective above it, names explicit communication (write, present, audience, rhetoric) or visual and multimodal work (visual, media, design). Genres (thesis, report), curricular sites (capstone, conference), and cognitive verbs (analyze, explain) are recorded and never counted, since each names a product, a place, or a kind of thinking without the act in which a student communicates it.

Part 4 · PolicyWhat peers and the field say

About 30 minutes, during the peer comparison, February through April.

These readings let the working group see what other departments and the field's societies have written, and where their wording created problems for the people following it, so the unit's own statement avoids the same problems.

This part is the peer comparison the proposal schedules for February through April. CWSP has read the AI statements of peer STS programs and the field's organizations, and the working group adds programs its members know.

In brief. No STS department among NC State's peers has an AI policy of its own, and neither of the field's two societies (4S and SHOT) has issued one. The nearest examples come from other universities and take four different forms, so NC State STS is free to choose the form its own statement takes.

The full finding and each document (7)

CWSP read the unit-level record at NC State's twelve official peers and the land-grant set used for FER, then the disciplinary societies and the nearest STS-type departments outside both sets. For each document, the entry gives its scope and CWSP's reading of where its language breaks down, offered for the working group to correct.

At the unit level the peer record is silent, since the two STS units among NC State's peers (Virginia Tech and UC Davis) have issued no AI policy and defer to campus guidance. Neither 4S nor SHOT has taken a position, although both now host panels on generative AI in teaching and research. The nearest unit-level texts come from outside the peer set, where departments have chosen four different genres: a set of default rules (Stony Brook), a clause folding AI into plagiarism (Georgia Tech), a declaration of principle for the humanities and social sciences (Cambridge), and a prohibition with a dissertation affirmation (Columbia History). NC State STS would write the first STS-specific statement among its peers, with the choice of genre open.

How to read a policy for where its language breaks

When authors write governance documents under institutional pressure, the language records more than they intended. Look for hedging (strongly discouraged), contradictions between sections or between two documents on one campus, silences where governance should be explicit, frameworks borrowed beyond their scope, and dates (language accurate when written and no longer true). The full method and the AI Governance Census are in the AI Policy Workbook (opens in a new window).

Use
Active and reflective use. Students use AI for the act, examine what the tool did and what it carries (whose data, labor, and energy), and disclose it.
Limit
Students use AI for named parts of the act and reflect on what it supplied, and they do the rest themselves, so the judgment the degree certifies stays with the student instead of passing to the tool.
Refuse
Students do the act without AI, and the program states its reason, which in STS includes the material and social costs of the tool.

Products

Each decision becomes one of four products. Insert a frame, then rewrite it in your program's words.

Five principles for durable policy language
  1. Scope terms by function. Define AI by what it does (generates text, produces images, automates tasks), since product names date within a term.
  2. Date the policy. State the term the policy covers and commit to revision.
  3. Name the revision trigger. Say what would prompt an update: new tools, new institutional guidance, or evidence of the policy producing the wrong outcomes.
  4. Separate principle from prohibition. State the commitment (what the program develops and assesses) apart from the rule (which functions are barred this term), because the commitment outlasts any one term's rule.
  5. Give the reason. A policy with its reason is easier to follow, and a later reviewer knows what to keep when the tools change.

Revised outcome

For an outcome naming only the thinking. Name the act, the object, the audience, and the student's accountability for AI decisions.

Assignment-level AI policy

Write one policy for each assignment, with its reason, since a general syllabus clause gives students no reason tied to the task.

Rubric criterion for reasoning about use

The criterion grades the student's reasoning about use, so a student who refuses AI and gives reasons meets it.

Program philosophy of use

One page recording the program's decisions and their reasons, kept as the Lab's written record.
