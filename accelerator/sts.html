Science, Technology and Society: AI Literacy and Policy Workbook

For the STS working group and its chair. The workbook takes the unit from its filed learning outcomes to written decisions about AI, one outcome at a time. Your baseline and the policy findings are already filled in.

What this workbook is

The Accelerator asks each unit one question per outcome: where students show the outcome, what they do there, what a model now does to the act, and what the program decides about it. The order is the method. An outcome naming the thinking and not the act cannot tell a student's work from a model's, so the silent outcome and the AI question are the same question.

  1. Literacy. Read the three levels (describe, analyze, decide) in your discipline's terms.
  2. Baseline. Check CWSP's reading of your filed outcomes.
  3. Map. For each outcome, name the course, the assessor, the act, and what a model does to it. January sessions.
  4. Policy. Read what peer units and the field have written, and where their language breaks.
  5. Lab. Decide, give the reason, and name the product. February and March sessions.
How saving works

Everything you type stays in this browser. Use Save and email me a link to continue on another computer or to hand the draft to a colleague, who opens it from the link. Anyone holding the link sees the draft. Submit sends the finished workbook to CWSP and emails you a copy. The unit's Google Sheet worksheet (opens in a new window) stays the record of decisions. The spreadsheet download at the end carries your rows into it.

Who you are

Part 1 · LiteracyThree levels, in STS

Each row of the worksheet runs three levels in order. CWSP's reading of each level in your discipline sits below its prompt. Correct the reading where it misdescribes the field.

Describe

In STS the system under description is also the object of study. A large language model predicts likely text from a corpus assembled by particular firms, trained with particular labor, and run on particular infrastructure. Describing it in the unit's own terms means naming the acts the outcomes ask of students (tracing a technology across accounts, explaining a concept, applying a method) and saying which of those acts a model performs on the surface.

List what students write, say, build, or present in the program. These become column E.

Analyze

The structural question is the field's home ground: whose data, labor, energy, and interests the tool carries into a student's analysis, and what a generated synthesis leaves out of an account of a sociotechnical system. The proposed STS 214 unit on AI's material and systemic dimensions places this analysis in the curriculum as content. The Accelerator asks the unit to apply the same analysis to its own assignments.

Decide

The operative question is which acts are the judgment the STS degree certifies, and what the program says about a tool supplying the surface of those acts. Refusal with a reason is a decision. A decision without a reason is unfinished.

Name two or three. The Lab returns to these.

Part 4 · PolicyWhat peers and the field say

The run read the unit-level record at NC State's twelve official peers and the land-grant set used for FER, then the disciplinary societies, then the nearest STS-type departments outside both sets. Each entry gives what the document governs and CWSP's reading of where its language breaks down. The readings are offered for the group to correct.

Finding. The peer record is silent at the unit level. The two STS units among NC State's peers, Virginia Tech and UC Davis, have issued no AI policy and defer to campus guidance. Neither 4S nor SHOT has issued a position, though both now host panels on generative AI in teaching and research. The nearest unit-level texts come from outside the peer set, and they take four different genres: a set of default rules (Stony Brook), a clause folding AI into plagiarism (Georgia Tech), a declaration of principle for the humanities and social sciences (Cambridge), and a prohibition with a dissertation affirmation (Columbia History). NC State STS would write the first STS-specific statement among its peers, and the choice of genre is open.

What it says
Seven default rules for courses and department work: attribution with software, version, and prompts, no wholesale substitution, limited use in serious academic work, students able to explain their work in person, and the policy on every syllabus.
Where the language breaks
A document stating it is updated regularly carries no date, so a reader cannot tell which edition governs a given term. The phrase limiting use in serious academic work leaves the line between serious and other work to the reader.

How to read a policy for where its language breaks

A governance document written under institutional pressure says more than its authors intended. Look for hedging (strongly discouraged), contradictions between sections or between two documents on one campus, silences where governance should be explicit, frameworks borrowed beyond their scope, and dates (language accurate when written and no longer true). The full method and the AI Governance Census are in the AI Policy Workbook (opens in a new window).

Products

Each decision becomes one of four products. Insert a frame, then rewrite it in your program's words.

Five principles for durable policy language
  1. Scope terms by function. Define AI by what it does (generates text, produces images, automates tasks). Product names date quickly.
  2. Date the policy. State the term the policy covers and commit to revision.
  3. Name the revision trigger. Say what would prompt an update: new tools, new institutional guidance, or evidence of the policy producing the wrong outcomes.
  4. Separate principle from prohibition. State the commitment (what the program develops and assesses) apart from the rule (which functions are barred this term). The first outlasts the second.
  5. Give the reason. A policy with its reason is easier to follow, and a later reviewer knows what to keep when the tools change.

Revised outcome

For an outcome naming only the thinking. Name the act, the object, the audience, and the student's accountability for AI decisions.

Assignment-level AI policy

One policy per assignment, with its reason. A general clause in the syllabus does not do this work.

Rubric criterion for reasoning about use

Grades the reasoning, not the choice. Refusal with reasons meets the criteria.

Program philosophy of use

The program-level account of decisions, one page. The Lab's written record.
