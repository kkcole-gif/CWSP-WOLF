Science, Technology and Society: AI Literacy and Policy Workbook

The STS working group and its chair use this workbook to move from the unit's filed learning outcomes to written decisions about AI, one outcome at a time, with the baseline and the policy findings already filled in by CWSP.

What this workbook is

For each outcome the Accelerator asks where students show it, what they do there, what a model now does to the act, and what the program decides about it. Since an outcome naming the thinking without the act gives a reader no way to tell a student's work from a model's, the outcome silent in the record and the AI question turn out to be one question, which is why the parts run in this order.

  1. Literacy. Read the three levels (describe, analyze, decide) in your discipline's terms.
  2. Baseline. Check CWSP's reading of your filed outcomes.
  3. Map. For each outcome, name the course, the assessor, the act, and what a model does to it (January sessions).
  4. Policy. Read what peer units and the field have written, and where their language breaks.
  5. Lab. Decide, give the reason, and name the product (February and March sessions).

Before the first session, read Parts 1, 2, and 4 and answer their prompts. The working group fills in Part 3 together in January and Part 5 in the Lab. To submit, the group needs at least one outcome mapped in Part 3.

Who you are

Part 1 · LiteracyThree levels, in STS

Each row of the worksheet runs three levels in order. Above each prompt is CWSP's reading of the level in your discipline, which the working group corrects wherever it misdescribes the field.

Describe

In STS the system under description is also the object of study. A large language model predicts likely text from a corpus assembled by particular firms, trained with particular labor, and run on particular infrastructure. Describing it in the unit's own terms means naming the acts the outcomes ask of students (tracing a technology across accounts, explaining a concept, applying a method) and saying which of those acts a model performs on the surface.

List what students write, say, build, or present in the program. These acts return in Part 3, one per outcome.

Analyze

The structural question is the field's home ground: whose data, labor, energy, and interests the tool carries into a student's analysis, and what a generated synthesis leaves out of an account of a sociotechnical system. The proposed STS 214 unit on AI's material and systemic dimensions places this analysis in the curriculum as content. The Accelerator asks the unit to apply the same analysis to its own assignments.

Decide

The operative question is which acts are the judgment the STS degree certifies, and what the program says about a tool supplying the surface of those acts. For each outcome the Lab records the decision and the reason the program gives for it, counting refusal with a reason as a decision.

Name two or three, which the Lab returns to.

How the codebook counts

CWSP counts an outcome as documenting communication when the outcome, or the objective above it, names explicit communication (write, present, audience, rhetoric) or visual and multimodal work (visual, media, design). Genres (thesis, report), curricular sites (capstone, conference), and cognitive verbs (analyze, explain) are recorded and never counted, since each names a product, a place, or a kind of thinking without the act in which a student communicates it.

Part 4 · PolicyWhat peers and the field say

CWSP read the unit-level record at NC State's twelve official peers and the land-grant set used for FER, then the disciplinary societies and the nearest STS-type departments outside both sets. For each document, the entry gives its scope and CWSP's reading of where its language breaks down, offered for the working group to correct.

Finding. At the unit level the peer record is silent, since the two STS units among NC State's peers (Virginia Tech and UC Davis) have issued no AI policy and defer to campus guidance. Neither 4S nor SHOT has taken a position, although both now host panels on generative AI in teaching and research. The nearest unit-level texts come from outside the peer set, where departments have chosen four different genres: a set of default rules (Stony Brook), a clause folding AI into plagiarism (Georgia Tech), a declaration of principle for the humanities and social sciences (Cambridge), and a prohibition with a dissertation affirmation (Columbia History). NC State STS would write the first STS-specific statement among its peers, with the choice of genre open.

What it says
Seven default rules for courses and department work: attribution with software, version, and prompts, no wholesale substitution, limited use in serious academic work, students able to explain their work in person, and the policy on every syllabus.
Where the language breaks
Although the department says it updates the page regularly, it gives no date, so readers have no way to tell which edition governs a given term. Because the rule limits use in "serious academic work" without defining the phrase, each reader draws the line alone.

How to read a policy for where its language breaks

When authors write governance documents under institutional pressure, the language records more than they intended. Look for hedging (strongly discouraged), contradictions between sections or between two documents on one campus, silences where governance should be explicit, frameworks borrowed beyond their scope, and dates (language accurate when written and no longer true). The full method and the AI Governance Census are in the AI Policy Workbook (opens in a new window).

Products

Each decision becomes one of four products. Insert a frame, then rewrite it in your program's words.

Five principles for durable policy language
  1. Scope terms by function. Define AI by what it does (generates text, produces images, automates tasks), since product names date within a term.
  2. Date the policy. State the term the policy covers and commit to revision.
  3. Name the revision trigger. Say what would prompt an update: new tools, new institutional guidance, or evidence of the policy producing the wrong outcomes.
  4. Separate principle from prohibition. State the commitment (what the program develops and assesses) apart from the rule (which functions are barred this term), because the commitment outlasts any one term's rule.
  5. Give the reason. A policy with its reason is easier to follow, and a later reviewer knows what to keep when the tools change.

Revised outcome

For an outcome naming only the thinking. Name the act, the object, the audience, and the student's accountability for AI decisions.

Assignment-level AI policy

Write one policy for each assignment, with its reason, since a general syllabus clause gives students no reason tied to the task.

Rubric criterion for reasoning about use

The criterion grades the student's reasoning about use, so a student who refuses AI and gives reasons meets it.

Program philosophy of use

One page recording the program's decisions and their reasons, kept as the Lab's written record.
