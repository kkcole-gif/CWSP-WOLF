AI in Writing Instruction

This elective introduces teaching writing in courses where students have AI tools at hand. It centers on one distinction that clarifies, for students and instructors alike, what AI is doing in the composing process. Plan on 3 to 4 hours.

What this elective asks you to do

  1. Compare writing WITH AI and writing FOR AI by completing the same task both ways. (Activity 1)
  2. Write a course AI policy statement for one course. (Activity 2)
  3. Redesign one assignment so WITH engagement becomes visible and assessable. (Activity 2)
  4. Evaluate AI detection tools as an assessment instrument. (Activity 3, optional)
Core Framework

Writing WITH AI, Not FOR AI

For writing instructors, the question that matters most is how students use AI. The WITH/FOR distinction (Cole) names two kinds of use with different consequences for learning and for rhetoric. Writing WITH AI uses the tool to extend your thinking. Writing FOR AI hands the thinking to the tool.

The framework applies to instructors who design assignments and to students who complete them. If your course asks students to use AI thoughtfully, it also needs to say what "thoughtfully" means.

Writing WITH AI

AI as Thinking Partner

  • Brainstorm ideas, then select, reject, or revise them on your own judgment
  • Use AI to generate an outline, then substantially restructure or depart from it
  • Ask AI to challenge your argument; use the pushback to strengthen your position
  • Prompt AI for counterarguments, then decide which deserve incorporation
  • Generate multiple framings of a claim; choose one and articulate why
  • Use AI feedback on a draft as one reader among several
Writing FOR AI

AI as Producer

  • Submit AI-generated text with minimal engagement or revision
  • Use AI to complete tasks without thinking them through
  • Accept AI phrasing, structure, or framing without questioning it
  • Prompt AI to write the assignment, then copy-edit the result
  • Treat AI output as a draft that needs only polishing
  • Use AI to skip the hard parts of writing

The WITH/FOR distinction leaves decisions about what to permit or prohibit to you. Use it as an analytical tool. It helps students see what kind of engagement they are having with a task. It helps you design assignments in which WITH engagement is the point.

Where the Learning Happens

Writing is a way of thinking. Students learn while they draft, revise, and reconsider, and the final submission records that work. When students write FOR AI, they skip that process. When students write WITH AI in an assignment designed for it, the tool extends and tests their thinking. Ask of any assignment: where does the thinking happen, and who does it?

Equity Dimensions

AI, Language, and Access

The WITH/FOR framework carries particular stakes for students whose language backgrounds differ from the dominant academic register. AI creates a real tension for these students. Be ready to name it openly with your class.

Two Considerations in Tension

AI flattens linguistic diversity. Large language models learn from web text that overrepresents some speakers, including, as Bender, Gebru, McMillan-Major, and Shmitchell (2021) note, younger users and those in developed countries. In practice, AI tools tend to return prose in the conventions of standard academic English. When students, multilingual writers among them, use AI to "fix" their prose, the output often strips out the distinctive rhetorical moves and linguistic features of their writing. The result is grammatically conventional and rhetorically thin. The writing starts to sound like a genre instead of a person.

AI helps students with language barriers. For students writing in an additional language, AI tools for grammar checking, clarification, and translation work as a real accommodation. The same tool that flattens one writer's voice helps another writer enter academic genres she is still learning. Both facts hold. A good AI policy acknowledges both.

Instructors working with multilingual students should read the WITH/FOR framework alongside Elective 4: Inclusive Practices for Multilingual Writers, which treats these questions at greater length, including the code-meshing vs. code-switching debate and the minimal marking principle.

Activity 1 · 60 minutes

WITH and FOR: The Same Task Twice

Individual reflection, 300–400 word comparative analysis

Complete one writing task twice with the same AI tool, once in a WITH mode and once in a FOR mode. Then compare how your own thinking went in each round.

The Task
Choose an assignment prompt from one of your current courses.
Pick a prompt you know well. It should call for an argument, an analysis, or an explanation that goes beyond summary. You are testing your own composing process, so choose a topic on which you hold views of your own.
Round 1: Writing FOR AI (15–20 min)

Prompt AI to complete the task. Review the output, make light edits for grammar and flow, and treat the result as your response. Accept the ideas as given.

Round 2: Writing WITH AI (20–25 min)

Use AI as a thinking partner. Brainstorm with it, challenge it, and have it argue against your position. Generate an outline, then depart from it substantially. Write your own response. Let the AI output inform it while the decisions stay yours.

What a complete response does

  • Describes decisions you made in the WITH task and the AI did not.
  • Names where your thinking stopped in the FOR task.
  • Draws one principle for assignment design from the comparison.

Prompts marked * need at least one full sentence each to submit the elective.

Activity 2 · 60–75 minutes

Write a Course AI Policy and Redesign One Assignment

Two-part writing task: policy statement + assignment redesign

A course AI policy is a teaching statement. It tells students what you believe about writing, learning, and AI. It also sets the context in which your assignments make sense. A policy that only lists what is permitted and prohibited leaves most of that work undone.

What a Good AI Policy Does

A good course AI policy names the WITH/FOR distinction, directly or by implication. It ties AI use to the course's learning goals and says what academic integrity means in this course. It also anticipates the equity questions a blanket policy ignores. Write it to students as thinkers who make their own choices.

Part A: Write Your Course AI Policy
A statement to appear in your course syllabus

Write a policy for a specific course you teach or will teach. Aim for 150–250 words, long enough to state your teaching values and short enough that students read it.

What a complete response does

  • States permitted and prohibited uses of AI and the reason for each.
  • Addresses students directly in 150 to 250 words.
  • Names the FOR path of least resistance in the original assignment.
  • Redesigns the assignment so students make and show their own decisions.

Prompts marked * need at least one full sentence each to submit the elective.

Part B: Redesign One Assignment
Identify an existing assignment and redesign it for WITH engagement

Choose an assignment from the same course. Work out how a student would complete it in a FOR mode. Then redesign it so that WITH engagement becomes possible and visible.

Activity 3 · Optional · 30 minutes

Evaluate an AI-Generated Response Scenario

Short analysis and policy reasoning exercise

Read the scenario below and respond to the questions. There is no required length. Write until you have said what you think.

Scenario
The Revised Draft

A first-year composition student submits a 1,200-word argumentative essay. You notice the prose is unusually polished for this student's previous work: confident transitions, varied sentence rhythm, strong claim structure. You run the text through an AI detection tool and receive a result of 78% "likely AI-generated." The student, when asked, says: "I used ChatGPT to help me brainstorm and organize my ideas, and then I rewrote everything in my own words. I thought that was okay. You didn't say we couldn't."

What a complete response does

  • Says whether your policy covers the scenario, and what you would add.
  • Names what detection tools measure and what they miss.
  • States the goal of your response to the student.
