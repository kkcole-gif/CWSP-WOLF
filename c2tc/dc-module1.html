Theoretical Foundation

What Digital Rhetoric Is and Why It Matters for Teaching

Your students grew up online. They read, watch, and post across several platforms every day. Few of them have been taught to think rhetorically about any of it: to ask why a text takes the form it does, how a platform shapes a message, or how writing for a networked public differs from writing for a single reader.

Digital rhetoric extends classical rhetorical theory (audience, purpose, ethos, pathos, logos) to the features of digital environments: hypertext, networked distribution, algorithmic curation, multimodality, and the blurred line between those who produce content and those who consume it. The field applies an ancient discipline to new conditions.

Four features set digital environments apart from print:

Multimodality

Digital texts combine written language, images, audio, data, and interactive elements. Each mode has its own rhetorical affordances, and the way a writer relates the modes is also a rhetorical choice.

Networked Distribution

Readers share and remix digital texts and strip them of context in ways print does not allow. The audience always extends beyond the intended reader.

Platform Architecture

A platform's character limits, recommendation algorithms, visual hierarchies, and monetization models work as rhetorical systems. They shape what people say on the platform and how they say it.

The fourth feature, the blurred line between producer and consumer, shapes what you need to teach. Students are at once the audience and the authors of digital texts. Comfort with a platform does not give them rhetorical awareness of it. Explicit instruction supplies that awareness.

What to teach

Students already know how to use platforms. Teach them to analyze the rhetorical choices built into what they do online, and to make those choices on purpose in disciplinary and professional settings.

Key Concepts

Platform Affordances, Hypertext, and Disciplinary Digital Communication

A platform affordance is what a platform makes easy, difficult, rewarded, or impossible. X's (formerly Twitter's) character limit leaves little room for qualification. YouTube's recommendation algorithm favors what holds viewers' attention, whether or not it is accurate. Instagram's visual economy shapes which bodies, lifestyles, and aesthetics circulate as normal. Each of these features is a rhetorical system, built into the platform's design and business model.

Hypertext changes the rhetorical situation in ways writers often overlook. A claim with a link performs a different rhetorical act from the same claim without one. The link promises verification and ties the text to an outside authority. It also invites readers to check, which changes how the claim works even for readers who never click.

Digital communication differs from field to field. Scientists post to preprint servers, historians maintain public digital archives, and engineers document projects on professional platforms. Each is a discipline-specific rhetorical practice, and each now occupies a growing share of professional life. Part of your work is to show students what digital communication looks like in your field.

Platform critique

To teach platform critique alongside platform fluency, ask two questions. What does this platform make easy or reward? Whose communicative practices does that design favor? Students who answer them learn to recognize the rhetorical defaults of digital environments and to decide whether to follow them.

AI Systems as Platform Actors: A Preview of Module 3

Recommendation algorithms, large language models, and AI content moderation tools now run inside the platforms where digital communication happens. These systems act rhetorically. They shape what circulates, what counts as credible, and whose communicative practices gain or lose an audience. To analyze a platform's affordances, you now need to identify which ones AI systems produce and how that changes whom the platform serves. Module 3 of this certificate gives you the full framework for that analysis. For now, carry one question forward: when you describe a platform's rhetorical effects, which of them does an AI system produce?

Activity 1

Rhetorical Analysis of a Digital Text

Individual analysis · 60 minutes · 350–450 words

Select a digital text from your discipline or profession, one you might assign or one students in your field will meet. Read it as a rhetorician, attending to how it works as well as what it says. Options include a discipline-relevant website, a data visualization with narrative framing, a public-facing research summary, a professional organization's digital publication, or a science communication piece.

Examine the text along five dimensions: the rhetorical situation (author, audience, and purpose, both stated and performed), platform affordances (how the platform shapes what the text does), modal choices (how modes work together or in tension), rhetorical appeals (how the text establishes credibility, appeals to emotion, and uses evidence), and pedagogical implications (what students would need to know to produce something like it).

Part A: Text Identification and Rhetorical Situation
What is the text, and what is its rhetorical situation?

What a complete response does

  • Identifies the text, its author, and its implied audience.
  • Explains how the platform shapes the text’s reach and structure.
  • Explains how two or more modes work together or in tension.
  • Ties at least one rhetorical appeal to evidence in the text.
  • Names the skills students would need to produce a text like it.

Activity 2

Digital Communication in Your Discipline

Individual reflection · 45 minutes · 250–300 words

Every discipline has its own digital communication practices. This activity asks you to map them in your field and to assess what your students know about them. The reflection becomes part of your rationale for the assignment you will design in the artifact builder below.

What a complete response does

  • Names two or three digital genres practitioners in your field use.
  • Describes one change in the last decade and what drove it.
  • States where students now learn these genres.

Resources

Module 1 Readings

  • ·Carter, Genesea M., and Aurora Matzke. "The More Digital Technology, the Better." In Bad Ideas About Writing, ed. Ball and Loewe. WVU Libraries (open access). A critical examination of uncritical techno-enthusiasm in education; establishes why more technology does not equal more learning.
  • ·Leverenz, Carrie S. "Design Thinking and the Wicked Problem of Teaching Writing." Computers and Composition, 33 (2014). Introduces design thinking as a framework for digital communication; treats digital composition as a design problem requiring iteration.
  • ·Daniel-Wariya, Joshua. "Gamification Makes Writing Fun." In Bad Ideas About Writing, edited by Cheryl E. Ball and Drew M. Loewe. WVU Libraries, 2017 (open access). A critique of gamified writing instruction from a scholar of play and procedural rhetoric.
  • ·Writing Spaces, Vol. 1–3. Parlor Press (open access, writingspaces.org). Chapters on digital rhetoric, networked writing, and multimodal communication, searchable by topic.
  • ·Cohn, Jenae. "Promoting Metacognitive Thought through Response to Low-Stakes Reflective Writing." Journal of Response to Writing 2.1 (2016). Useful for designing reflective components that make students' rhetorical decisions visible.
