What WOLF is

WOLF works with departments, programs, and colleges to develop discipline-specific approaches to writing and speaking instruction. The goal is to make the communication instruction already in your curriculum visible and sustainable, fill in what is missing, and connect it to the outcomes your discipline already holds. The central product is a Writing and Speaking Curriculum Plan (WSCP), a document your department owns. Departments that complete the program earn CWSP certification and a $1,000 mini grant.

Stage 1 · InquiryWhere are we?CWSP meets with the department to assess current writing and speaking practices, identify what is working and what is missing, and confirm the faculty liaison. The stage produces a clear picture of the curriculum as it stands.
Stage 2 · DesignWhat should be different?The department, with CWSP support, develops the WSCP: outcomes for writing and speaking in the discipline, mapped to courses and levels, with assignment and assessment approaches. Faculty author it.
Stage 3 · EvidenceHow do we know it is working?The department implements the WSCP, collects assessment data, and uses it to refine the plan. Completion brings CWSP certification, recognition, and the mini grant.
Your role as liaison

You are the point of contact between your department and CWSP. You facilitate; you do not produce everything yourself. You connect CWSP to your colleagues, translate disciplinary context into curriculum decisions, and keep the work moving between consultations. You need institutional knowledge of your department and enough trust from colleagues to facilitate honest conversation about the curriculum. Expertise in writing pedagogy is CWSP's job.

CWSP handles the pedagogical scaffolding, provides templates and tools, co-leads workshops, and consults one-on-one. The WSCP that results is your department's document, owned, revised, and sustained by faculty, with CWSP as a continuing resource.

What WOLF is not

WOLF does not ask departments to add writing-intensive courses, require faculty to become writing or speaking specialists, or produce a plan that looks the same across disciplines. A Chemistry WSCP looks different from a Communication WSCP, by design. CWSP supports that specificity.

WOLF runs on no fixed calendar. Departments move through the stages at a pace that fits their context, and CWSP is available throughout.

Engaging your colleagues

Start with questions. Before presenting the WSCP as a project, ask colleagues what challenges they face with student writing and speaking, what they notice is missing, and what they wish students arrived knowing. Those conversations surface the disciplinary specificity a WSCP needs, and they build investment before any formal process begins.

Workload, grading burden, and skepticism about program initiatives deserve a hearing. You do not have to answer them. Relay them to CWSP and bring the responses back. Nominating a colleague for a Communication Champion letter early signals that WOLF recognizes work already being done.

The AI Literacies track

A department can enter WOLF through AI Literacies: structured consultations, policy review, and curricular mapping focused on how generative AI is governed and taught in the discipline. The track stands alone or opens into full WOLF participation, and it connects to Stage 2, where AI literacy outcomes join the WSCP. See AI Literacies, the faculty workbook, and the three-level framework.

Activity 3

Needs assessment

Faculty and student surveys · short and anonymous

Brief surveys, 10 to 12 questions, faculty and student versions, collect perceptions of current instruction and show where gaps are felt most. Google Forms exports to Sheets for analysis. CWSP has survey templates. Record what came back.

Sample questions

Faculty. Which courses in your teaching include a significant writing or speaking component? What do you find most difficult about teaching writing and speaking in your discipline? Where do you think students arrive least prepared?

Students. In which courses have you received substantial feedback on your writing or speaking this semester? What communication skill do you feel least confident about in your discipline? Where do you wish you had more instruction?

Patterns, with a few responses in faculty members' own words.

Where faculty and students agree, and where they diverge.

Stage 2 · Design

Design is where the Writing and Speaking Curriculum Plan gets written. Faculty author it; CWSP co-leads the workshops and consults between them; you keep it moving. The five activities below are the plan's five parts. Answer them in conversation with colleagues, over several sessions, and the plan assembles itself at the end of the stage. Bring any part to CWSP at any point.

A WSCP is specific to this discipline and this curriculum. Where a prompt asks for examples, the examples are prompts for conversation, and a Chemistry answer should look nothing like a Communication answer.

Activity 6 · WSCP Part 1

Your discipline's communication

Faculty conversation · one session · describe writing and speaking as they work in the field

Before mapping outcomes, say what writing and speaking do in your discipline: the genres, the readers and listeners, and what graduates are expected to produce. Write in the plural, since the answers belong to the department.

Academic and professional genres both. Research reports, policy briefs, case studies, clinical notes, grant proposals, technical memos.

Research presentations, client briefings, oral defenses, clinical communication, team facilitation, public testimony.

A policy brief assumes a different reader than a lab report. A client presentation runs on different norms than a conference paper.

Activity 7 · WSCP Part 2

Outcomes by level

Faculty working session with CWSP · the outcomes describe what students will be able to do, for whom, and in what contexts

One line per level is enough to start. Write outcomes in the discipline's terms. "Write clearly" is a general outcome; "write a methods section a reviewer in the field accepts without revision" belongs to a discipline. Number them as you go (W1, S1), so the map in Activity 8 refers to them.

Pack Proficiencies, accreditation SLOs, or program outcomes already on record. Say where the WSCP outcomes extend them.

Scaffolding by level, for filling the gaps

Introductory. Orientation: students learn what writing and speaking look like in the field and practice at low stakes. Short response and reflection papers, lab or field reports with structured templates, annotated readings. Impromptu or short prepared presentations, structured participation protocols.

Intermediate. Disciplinary voice and argument: engaging sources, developing positions, addressing more than one audience. Research papers and literature reviews, case analyses, multi-draft assignments with structured revision. Group presentations, debates or structured argument.

Advanced and capstone. Professional communication for real audiences in real genres. Independent research papers and theses, professional documents aimed outside the classroom (grant proposals, policy briefs, technical memos, client reports), portfolios. Oral defenses, client or stakeholder presentations.

At every level. Structured peer review with four to six prompts tied to the criteria. Rubric-based self-evaluation before submission. A revision memo on multi-draft work saying what changed and why.
