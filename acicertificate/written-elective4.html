Inclusive Practices for Multilingual Writers

How you read, respond to, and support student writing depends on what you assume about linguistic difference. This elective brings those assumptions into view and offers frameworks for reading multilingual writing as evidence of what students know and do. Plan on 3 to 4 hours.

Framework

Asset-Based and Deficit-Based Orientations

The first choice in multilingual writing pedagogy concerns what you believe a text shows. Before you decide how to respond to a multilingual writer, you have already decided, usually without noticing, what that writer's text represents. Is linguistic difference a problem to correct, or a resource to understand?

Asset-Based Framework

Linguistic Difference as Resource

  • Multilingual writers bring rhetorical repertoires that monolingual writers lack
  • Non-standard features are often intentional or systematic
  • Transfer from other languages shapes argument structure, evidence use, and sense of audience in ways readers in the discipline recognize
  • Students' home languages and dialects are legitimate intellectual and rhetorical resources
  • Instruction adds registers to the student's repertoire and keeps the ones already there
  • Instructor response attends to meaning, argument, and rhetorical purpose
Deficit-Based Framework

Linguistic Difference as Problem

  • Non-standard features are errors to be marked and corrected
  • The goal is conformity to dominant academic register
  • Surface-level features are treated as primary concerns, regardless of content
  • Students' prior linguistic knowledge is treated as interference
  • Writing instruction aims to eliminate features
  • Instructor response puts correctness ahead of communication

The deficit framework also works against learning. Research on error correction in second-language writing (Ferris; Truscott, discussed below) questions whether correcting surface features without attention to meaning improves writing. Correction of that kind pulls student attention away from argument and toward surface compliance. The asset-based framework still addresses surface features. It ranks them below the rhetorical and intellectual work of the text.

In practice

An asset-based instructor reads a multilingual student's text and asks first: What is the argument? What evidence does the writer use? How does the writer understand the audience? After answering these questions, the instructor turns to surface features, and then only to those that block communication. Features that differ from dominant conventions while staying clear are left alone.

Framework

Code-Switching and Code-Meshing

The terms "code-switching" and "code-meshing" name different theories of how multilingual writers relate to their linguistic repertoires, and they imply different pedagogical programs.

Code-Switching

Separation of Codes

This view holds that linguistic codes (home language, academic English, disciplinary register) belong apart, and that students must learn to "switch" between them as context requires. Academic writing calls for academic English. Home language and dialect belong to other settings.

In the classroom: Students learn to recognize which register a context demands and to use it. Their home language is acknowledged and kept out of academic writing. The dominant register is presented as a neutral requirement, which hides that it reflects cultural and political choices.
Code-Meshing

Integration of Codes

This view, associated with scholars including Young and Canagarajah, holds that writers draw on several linguistic codes at once, and legitimately so. On this account, the separation code-switching demands is artificial, performative, and often impossible to sustain. Code-meshing treats multilingual repertoires as resources for composing.

In the classroom: Instructors recognize that features from students' home languages sometimes appear in academic writing as rhetorical choices. The instructor asks "does this serve the text?" Students are not asked to perform a linguistic identity they do not hold.

The debate between code-switching and code-meshing remains open. You do not need to adopt one position whole. Awareness of both matters most. An instructor who has never considered code-meshing is more likely to mark legitimate rhetorical choices as errors and to read linguistic difference as deficiency.

Principle

The Minimal Marking Principle

Research on error correction in L2 writing (Ferris; Truscott) raises a troubling possibility. Correcting every surface error in student writing might not improve writing over time. Truscott argues it does harm, by turning student attention from content to compliance.

Mark patterns

Under minimal marking, instructors identify and respond to patterns of error and leave individual instances unmarked. If a student regularly confuses the definite and indefinite article, marking every instance does not teach the rule. It produces a paper covered in marks, which discourages the student and wastes your time.

Identify the pattern, name it, explain it once with an example, and ask the student to find and revise the other instances. Error correction becomes active learning. The student does the work of recognizing and revising, and you supply the explanation.

Minimal marking also separates errors that block communication from features that differ from convention. When you read an L2 text, ask: does this feature keep me from understanding what the writer means? If it does not, it might need no correction at all. This holds especially in early drafts, where attention to argument and structure pays off more than surface editing.

The principle applies most directly to formative feedback. Summative assessment (a final grade) involves other considerations, and disciplinary conventions matter for students preparing to write professionally in a field. In drafting, comprehensive error marking is rarely the best instructional choice for any writer, multilingual or monolingual.

Activity 1 · 60–75 minutes

Reading Student Writing Through an L2 Lens

Three student writing excerpts, close reading and analysis

Read each excerpt below and respond to the questions. The goal is practice in asset-based reading: identify what the writer does well rhetorically before you turn to surface features.

Excerpt 1 Graduate student, Materials Engineering: nano-composite fabrication report
"The samples were subjected to heat treatment at elevated temperature for the purpose of achieving microstructural homogeneity, which is a necessary condition for the subsequent mechanical testing procedure. The results of the tensile strength measurements, they show a consistent pattern of improvement in the direction that was predicted by our hypothesis, although some deviation was observed at higher loading fractions which may indicate the presence of interfacial defects not accounted for in the theoretical model."
Excerpt 2 Undergraduate, Sociology: paper on debt and social obligation
"In Filipino culture there is a concept called utang na loob, which means a debt of the inside or inner debt. This is not same as financial debt: it is the debt you owe to persons who have given you something important, like your life or your education. My grandmother always say we must never forget this debt, because the person who forgets becomes hollow. I think this concept can help us understand why some communities in the United States do not trust formal financial institutions, because the relationship of trust is different and the obligation runs in different direction than what the bank assumes."
Excerpt 3 Upper-division, Public Health: policy brief on community health workers
"The community health worker (CHW) model has demonstrated effectiveness in reaching populations that the formal healthcare system cannot reach, because the CHW lives in the community and shares the language and experience of the patient. However, many CHW programs, they face funding instability because the value of this work is not legible to the metrics that funders use. The CHW does not produce a claim or a procedure code: she produces trust, and trust is not billable. This suggest that the policy problem is not about effectiveness but about translation: the program is effective, but its effectiveness is not visible in the language that funding decisions require."

Discipline-Specific Support Plan for Multilingual Writers

Two-part task: instructor support plan + student-facing version

Design support for multilingual writers in a course you teach or plan to teach. The plan should take an asset-based orientation and apply minimal marking to your feedback.

Activity 3 · 45 minutes

Writing a Substantive Response to an L2 Draft

Applied response writing exercise

Return to one of the three excerpts from Activity 1. Write to that student as if you were returning a draft, in the form you would use at the end of a paper or in a feedback document.

Take an asset-based orientation and apply minimal marking. Respond first to the intellectual and rhetorical work of the text. Address surface features only as the priorities you set in Activity 2 allow.

AI, Multilingual Writing, and the Deficit Trap

AI writing tools raise a particular concern for multilingual writers. When a student asks AI to "fix" or "improve" their prose, the tool often returns a standardized, unmarked academic register. That output erases the distinctive rhetorical features of the student's writing. The result is grammatically normative and rhetorically thin. An instructor who grades the AI-generated version approvingly, unaware of what was lost, tells the student that their own language falls short. An asset-based orientation includes explicit conversation with students about what AI editing does and does not do, and about whether "fixing" language improves writing.

The Writing WITH AI, Not FOR AI framework (Elective 3) applies directly here. Using AI to draft whole texts is a different practice from using AI as a reader or as a translator of specific terms. Help multilingual students tell these uses apart explicitly. Do not assume the distinction is obvious to them.
