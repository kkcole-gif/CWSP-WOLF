Inclusive Practices for Multilingual Writers

How instructors read, respond to, and support student writing depends substantially on the assumptions they bring to linguistic difference. This elective makes those assumptions visible and offers frameworks for replacing deficit orientations with asset-based approaches.

Framework

Asset-Based vs. Deficit-Based Orientations

The most fundamental choice in multilingual writing pedagogy is not methodological — it is epistemological. Before deciding how to respond to a multilingual writer's text, an instructor makes a prior decision, usually unconsciously, about what that text represents. Is linguistic difference a problem to be corrected, or a resource to be understood?

Asset-Based Framework

Linguistic Difference as Resource

  • Multilingual writers possess rhetorical repertoires that monolingual writers lack
  • Non-standard features may be intentional or systematic, not random
  • Transfer from other languages shapes argument structure, evidence use, and audience orientation in ways that are disciplinarily legible
  • Students' home languages and dialects are legitimate intellectual and rhetorical resources
  • The goal is to expand students' repertoire, not to erase one register in favor of another
  • Instructor response focuses on meaning-making, argumentation, and rhetorical purpose
Deficit-Based Framework

Linguistic Difference as Problem

  • Non-standard features are errors to be marked and corrected
  • The goal is conformity to dominant academic register
  • Surface-level features are treated as primary concerns, regardless of content
  • Students' prior linguistic knowledge is treated as interference rather than resource
  • Writing instruction focuses on eliminating features rather than expanding capacities
  • Instructor response prioritizes correctness over communication

The deficit framework is not merely unkind — it is pedagogically counterproductive. Research in second-language acquisition consistently shows that error correction focused on surface features without attention to meaning does not improve writing; it redirects student attention away from argumentation and toward surface compliance. The asset-based framework does not ignore surface features, but it treats them as secondary to the rhetorical and intellectual work of the text.

What This Means in Practice

An asset-based instructor reads a multilingual student's text and asks first: What is the argument? What evidence does the writer use? How does the writer understand their audience? Only after engaging these questions does the instructor turn to surface features — and then only those that impede communication, not those that merely differ from dominant conventions.

Framework

Code-Switching and Code-Meshing

The terms "code-switching" and "code-meshing" name different theories of how multilingual writers relate to their linguistic repertoires, and they imply different pedagogical programs.

Code-Switching

Separation of Codes

The view that different linguistic codes (home language, academic English, disciplinary register) are properly kept separate, and that students must learn to "switch" between them depending on context. Academic writing requires academic English; home language and dialect are appropriate in other contexts.

Pedagogical implication: Students are taught to recognize which register a context demands and to deploy it. Their home language is acknowledged but sequestered from academic writing. The dominant register is treated as a neutral requirement rather than a cultural and political choice.
Code-Meshing

Integration of Codes

The view, associated with scholars including Young and Canagarajah, that writers naturally and legitimately draw on multiple linguistic codes simultaneously — that the "separation" demanded by code-switching is artificial, performative, and often impossible in practice. Code-meshing treats multilingual repertoires as compositional resources.

Pedagogical implication: Instructors recognize that linguistic features from students' home languages may appear in academic writing not as errors but as rhetorical choices. The question is not "is this standard?" but "does this serve the text?" Students are not asked to perform a linguistic identity they do not hold.

The code-switching/code-meshing debate is not resolved, and instructors need not adopt one position entirely. What matters is awareness: an instructor who has never considered the code-meshing perspective is more likely to mark legitimate rhetorical choices as errors, and more likely to respond to linguistic difference as deficiency rather than difference.

Principle

The Minimal Marking Principle

Research on error correction in L2 writing (Ferris; Truscott) raises a troubling possibility: comprehensive correction of surface-level errors in student writing may not improve writing over time, and may actively harm it by redirecting student attention from content to compliance, and by producing learned helplessness in revision.

Mark Patterns, Not Instances

The minimal marking principle holds that instructors should identify and respond to patterns of error rather than marking every instance of a recurring feature. If a student consistently confuses the definite and indefinite article, marking every instance does not help them understand the rule — it produces a paper covered in marks, which is both discouraging and pedagogically inefficient.

Instead: identify the pattern, name it, explain it once with an example, and ask the student to locate and revise additional instances themselves. This turns error correction into an active learning task. The student does the cognitive work of recognition and revision; the instructor provides the analytical framework.

Minimal marking also means distinguishing between errors that impede communication and features that merely differ from convention. An instructor reading through an L2 text should ask: does this feature prevent me from understanding what the writer means? If not, it may not warrant correction at all — especially in early drafts, where attending to argumentation and structure is more productive than surface editing.

This principle applies most directly to formative feedback. Summative assessment — a final grade — involves different considerations, and disciplinary conventions matter for students preparing to write professionally in a field. But in the drafting process, comprehensive error marking is rarely the most effective instructional choice for any writer, multilingual or not.

Activity 1 · 60–75 minutes

Reading Student Writing Through an L2 Lens

Three student writing excerpts, close reading and analysis

Read each excerpt below and respond to the questions. Your goal is to practice reading multilingual writing through an asset-based lens — identifying what the writer is doing well rhetorically before attending to surface features.

Excerpt 1 Graduate student, Materials Engineering — nano-composite fabrication report
"The samples were subjected to heat treatment at elevated temperature for the purpose of achieving microstructural homogeneity, which is a necessary condition for the subsequent mechanical testing procedure. The results of the tensile strength measurements, they show a consistent pattern of improvement in the direction that was predicted by our hypothesis, although some deviation was observed at higher loading fractions which may indicate the presence of interfacial defects not accounted for in the theoretical model."
Excerpt 2 Undergraduate, Sociology — paper on debt and social obligation
"In Filipino culture there is a concept called utang na loob, which means a debt of the inside or inner debt. This is not same as financial debt — it is the debt you owe to persons who have given you something important, like your life or your education. My grandmother always say we must never forget this debt, because the person who forgets becomes hollow. I think this concept can help us understand why some communities in the United States do not trust formal financial institutions — because the relationship of trust is different and the obligation runs in different direction than what the bank assumes."
Excerpt 3 Upper-division, Public Health — policy brief on community health workers
"The community health worker (CHW) model has demonstrated effectiveness in reaching populations that the formal healthcare system cannot reach, because the CHW lives in the community and shares the language and experience of the patient. However, many CHW programs, they face funding instability because the value of this work is not legible to the metrics that funders use. The CHW does not produce a claim or a procedure code — she produces trust, and trust is not billable. This suggest that the policy problem is not about effectiveness but about translation: the program is effective, but its effectiveness is not visible in the language that funding decisions require."

Activity 2 · 60 minutes

Discipline-Specific Support Plan for Multilingual Writers

Two-part task: instructor support plan + student-facing version

Design a support structure for multilingual writers in a specific course you teach or plan to teach. This plan should reflect an asset-based orientation and apply the minimal marking principle to your feedback practices.

Activity 3 · 45 minutes

Writing a Substantive Response to an L2 Draft

Applied response writing exercise

Return to one of the three excerpts from Activity 1. Write a substantive written response to that student as if you were returning a draft — the kind of response you would actually write at the end of a paper or in a feedback document.

Your response should reflect your asset-based orientation and apply the minimal marking principle. It should engage first with the intellectual and rhetorical work of the text, and address surface features only according to the prioritization scheme you developed in Activity 2.

AI, Multilingual Writing, and the Deficit Trap

AI writing tools present a specific concern in multilingual writing contexts. When a student uses AI to "fix" or "improve" their prose, they frequently receive output in a standardized, unmarked academic register — output that erases the distinctive rhetorical features of their writing. The result can be grammatically normative but rhetorically thin. An instructor who then grades the AI-generated version approvingly, without awareness of what was lost, reinforces the message that the student's own language is insufficient. An asset-based orientation should include explicit conversation with students about what AI editing does and does not do — and whether "fixing" language is the same as improving writing.

The Writing WITH AI, Not FOR AI framework (Elective 3) is particularly relevant here: using AI to draft entire texts is a different practice than using AI as a reader or a translator of specific terms. Instructors may need to help multilingual students distinguish between these uses explicitly, rather than assuming the distinction is self-evident.
