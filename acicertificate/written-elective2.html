Writing Across the Curriculum Strategies

Low-stakes writing is a way of teaching course content. This elective lays out the framework behind Writing Across the Curriculum (WAC) and gives you a menu of short writing activities to add to a course you already teach, without redesigning it. Plan on 3 to 4 hours.

What this elective asks you to do

  1. Design one low-stakes writing activity for a named moment in your course. (Activity 1)
  2. Classify the activity as writing to learn, writing to engage, or writing in the disciplines. (Activity 1, prompt 2)
  3. Anticipate the logistical and teaching obstacles the activity will meet. (Activity 2)
  4. Plan three to five WAC activities across a course, with a grading approach for each. (WAC integration plan)
  5. Name the evidence you will use to judge whether the activities support learning. (Activity 2, prompt 3, and the plan)
Framework

Three Categories of WAC Activity

WAC distinguishes three kinds of writing, each with its own purpose. Decide which one you are designing for first. That choice shapes how you assign the writing, how you respond to it, and whether you grade it.

Writing-to-Learn (WTL)

Low-stakes writing that helps students work through and retain course content. The writer is the audience, and engagement counts for more than correctness. WTL is usually ungraded or graded on completion. Students use it to think on the page.

Writing-to-Engage (WTE)

Writing that builds conversation among students: in-class responses, discussion posts, peer commentary. WTE is written for exchange. Students write to each other as well as to themselves and the instructor.

Writing-in-Disciplines (WID)

Formal writing in disciplinary genres: the research paper, the lab report, the case analysis, the policy brief. You evaluate WID against your field's standards, and it usually carries high stakes. Module 3 of this certificate covers it in depth. WTL and WTE prepare students to do it well.

Grading writing-to-learn

Some writing needs no grade. Elbow argues that when students expect to be judged on correctness, they stop taking the risks exploratory writing depends on. Grade WTL on completion, or leave it ungraded. Students gain from the thinking they do while writing. Your rigor moves to the prompt you write and to the formal assignments that follow.

WAC activities and AI

Faculty often worry that exit tickets, minute papers, and freewrites are too easy to fake with AI. Look at what the prompt asks for. An exit ticket that says "summarize tonight's reading in your own words" is easy to fake, because it asks for nothing that happened in your class. An exit ticket that asks "what was the most surprising claim in today's discussion, and what prior assumption does it challenge for you?" is hard to answer without having been in the room. Prompts that ask for the particular, the local, and the personal hold up against AI substitution. They do so by requiring students to be present.

A Menu of WAC Activities

These seven activity types appear throughout WAC research and practice. Each entry gives the purpose and typical logistics. In Activity 1 you will choose one and design it in full.

Seven WAC Activity Types

Exit Ticket

2–3 minutes at class end. Students write one thing they learned, one thing they are still confused about, or one question they are carrying out. Purpose: consolidation and instructor feedback on comprehension. Grade: completion or ungraded.

Minute Paper

5–8 minutes at any pause in class. Students respond to a specific question about the material you have covered in the preceding minutes. It is more targeted than an exit ticket and focuses attention on one concept. Purpose: comprehension check and active processing. Grade: completion.

Pre-Class Reading Response

100–200 words before class. Students summarize what they read, identify the central argument, and bring one question. Purpose: preparation accountability and question generation. Feeds class discussion directly. Grade: completion or brief rubric (arrived with a real question).

Freewrite

5–10 minutes, no interruptions, no correction. Students write continuously on a prompt without stopping. Purpose: generating ideas and setting aside the urge to correct. Useful before drafting or before class discussion. Grade: ungraded.

Learning Log / Lab Notebook

Ongoing, 3–10 minutes per entry. Students document observations, questions, confusions, and connections over time. Purpose: metacognitive development and pattern recognition across the course. Grade: completion at set intervals.

Explain-It-to-a-Stranger

10–15 minutes. Students write an explanation of a complex concept for someone who knows nothing about it. Purpose: testing depth of understanding. Gaps in understanding show up when students try to explain a concept in plain terms. Grade: ungraded or completion.

Post-Lab / Post-Data Reflection

15–20 minutes after an empirical activity. Students write what they observed, what surprised them, what question their results raise, and what they would do differently. Purpose: connecting procedure to interpretation. Grade: completion or brief checkpoint.

Activity 1

Design a Low-Stakes WAC Activity

Individual design · 45–60 minutes

Choose one activity type from the menu and design it in full for a specific course and a specific point in the semester. Write the actual materials, complete enough that you or a colleague would be ready to use them in class.

Tie the activity to one moment in the course: a particular reading, discussion, or concept. A prompt written for that moment asks students to think about something specific. A prompt reused all semester tends to get generic answers.

Activity Design
Complete all five design elements.

  • Places the activity at a named moment in a named course.
  • Gives the exact prompt students will see.
  • States the time, setting, and grading approach.

Activity 2

WAC Implementation Reflection

Individual reflection · 30 minutes · 300–400 words

Think through how your Activity 1 design will play out in class, with attention to what is most likely to get in its way. Plan for those obstacles now, before they give you a reason to drop the activity mid-semester.

Most WAC activities that fail do so for logistical reasons. The activity takes longer than planned. Students do not take ungraded work seriously. The activity gets cut when class runs long. Name these problems in advance and plan around them.

What a complete response does

  • Names two or three logistical obstacles and a response to each.
  • Names one way the activity itself might fail its purpose.
  • Names evidence of learning beyond student engagement.

Prompts marked * are required to submit the elective.

Contribute to the Repository

Implementation reflections help later participants plan for obstacles before they meet them in class.

Your Artifact

Build a WAC Integration Plan

Draw on Activities 1 and 2 to write a semester-long plan for where and how you will add low-stakes writing to one of your courses. The plan is for your own use, and its format makes it easy to share with a department colleague or your CWSP liaison.

What a complete response does

  • Lists three to five activities with timing, type, and grading approach for each.
  • States how the activities count toward the course grade, and why.
  • Names the changes you will look for in student work.

Elective 2 Readings

  • ·Stevenson, Colleen M. "Benefits of a Low-Stakes Write-to-Learn Assignment on Exam Performance." Teaching of Psychology 47.1 (2020): 45–49. In a child development course, students who completed five short write-to-learn assignments outperformed a comparison section on two of three exams.
  • ·Leon, Adele. "Low-Stakes Writing as a High-Impact Education Practice in MBA Classes." A pilot study of low-stakes writing in two MBA courses, with interview data from faculty outside writing studies and survey data from students.
  • ·Freitas, Kripa. "Low-Stakes Writing in an Active-Learning Classroom Needs Focus and Feedback to Be Effective." Journal of Economic Education 54.3 (2023): 243–255. An economics study whose title states its finding: low-stakes writing helped learning when prompts were focused and students received feedback.
  • ·Elbow, Peter. "Writing for Learning, Not Just for Demonstrating Learning." Elbow's short statement of the distinction between low-stakes and high-stakes writing, written for faculty across disciplines.
  • ·Cunningham, Eamon. "Teaching Invention: Leveraging the Power of Low-Stakes Writing." Teaching/Writing: The Journal of Writing Teacher Education 6.1 (2019). A teacher's account of using low-stakes writing to help students generate and develop ideas.
