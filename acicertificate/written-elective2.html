Writing Across the Curriculum Strategies

Low-stakes writing is not a supplement to teaching — it is a form of teaching. This elective gives you the conceptual framework for WAC and a menu of practical, evidence-based strategies you can implement immediately without restructuring your course.

Framework

Three Categories of WAC Activity

Writing Across the Curriculum encompasses three distinct kinds of writing, each serving different learning purposes. Understanding which category you are designing for determines how you should assign, respond to, and grade student writing.

Writing-to-Learn (WTL)

Low-stakes writing that helps students process, consolidate, and engage with course content. The audience is the writer; correctness is secondary to engagement. WTL is typically ungraded or graded on completion. Its purpose is cognitive — to help students think by writing, not to demonstrate mastery to an evaluator.

Writing-to-Engage (WTE)

Writing that builds community and conversation — in-class responses, discussion posts, peer commentary. WTE is oriented toward exchange rather than learning-in-isolation. It is social and dialogic; students are writing for each other, not just for themselves or the instructor.

Writing-in-Disciplines (WID)

Formal writing in disciplinary genres — the research paper, the lab report, the case analysis, the policy brief. WID is evaluated against disciplinary standards and is typically high-stakes. It is the culminating genre for Module 3 of this certificate; WTL and WTE are the scaffolding that prepares students to do it well.

The Key Point About Grading

Not all writing must be graded. WTL is most effective when students do not feel evaluated on correctness — the evaluative pressure suppresses exactly the exploratory, risk-taking thinking WTL is designed to produce. Grade WTL on completion, or not at all. The pedagogical return comes from the thinking, not the product. This is not a reduction in rigor — it is a redistribution of where rigor belongs.

WAC Activities and AI — A Design Question

WAC's low-stakes writing activities — exit tickets, minute papers, freewrites — are often framed as "too easy to fake with AI." The question is worth inverting: an exit ticket that asks "summarize tonight's reading in your own words" is fakeable because it does not require actual engagement with the class's specific intellectual moment. An exit ticket that asks "what was the most surprising claim in today's discussion, and what prior assumption does it challenge for you?" is much harder to complete without having been in the room. WAC activity design that specifies the particular, the situated, and the personal is inherently more resistant to AI substitution — not because it polices for AI, but because it requires genuine presence.

Tools

A Menu of WAC Activities

The following activity types are among the most well-supported in WAC research and practice. Each is briefly described with its purpose and typical logistics. You will select one to design in full in Activity 1.

Seven Evidence-Based WAC Activity Types

Exit Ticket

2–3 minutes at class end. Students write one thing they learned, one thing they are still confused about, or one question they are carrying out. Purpose: consolidation and instructor feedback on comprehension. Grade: completion or ungraded.

Minute Paper

5–8 minutes at any class pause. Students respond to a specific question about the material just covered. More targeted than an exit ticket; designed to focus attention on a specific concept. Purpose: comprehension check and active processing. Grade: completion.

Pre-Class Reading Response

100–200 words before class. Students summarize what they read, identify the central argument, and bring one question. Purpose: preparation accountability and question generation. Feeds class discussion directly. Grade: completion or brief rubric (arrived with a real question).

Freewrite

5–10 minutes, no interruptions, no correction. Students write continuously on a prompt without stopping. Purpose: generating ideas and bypassing the inner editor. Particularly useful before drafting or before class discussion. Grade: ungraded.

Learning Log / Lab Notebook

Ongoing, 3–10 minutes per entry. Students document observations, questions, confusions, and connections over time. Purpose: metacognitive development and pattern recognition across the course. Grade: completion at set intervals.

Explain-It-to-a-Stranger

10–15 minutes. Students write an explanation of a complex concept for someone who knows nothing about it. Purpose: testing depth of understanding — if you cannot explain it simply, you do not understand it fully. Grade: ungraded or completion.

Post-Lab / Post-Data Reflection

15–20 minutes after an empirical activity. Students write what they observed, what surprised them, what question their results raise, and what they would do differently. Purpose: connecting procedure to interpretation. Grade: completion or brief checkpoint.

Activity 1

Design a Low-Stakes WAC Activity

Individual design · 45–60 minutes

Select one activity type from the menu above and design it in full for a specific course and moment in your curriculum. The design should be specific enough that you or a colleague could implement it directly — not a description of what you would do, but the actual materials.

The most effective WAC activities are tied to a specific moment in the course — a particular reading, discussion, or concept — rather than generic across the semester. Specificity is what makes them intellectually substantive.

Activity Design
Complete all five design elements.

Activity 2

WAC Implementation Reflection

Individual reflection · 30 minutes · 300–400 words

This activity asks you to think through the implementation of your Activity 1 design — specifically, the obstacles that are most likely to prevent it from working as designed. The goal is not to talk yourself out of it but to anticipate and address those obstacles before they become reasons to abandon the activity mid-semester.

The most common WAC implementation failures are not pedagogical but logistical: the activity takes longer than planned, students do not take ungraded work seriously, the activity gets cut when class runs long. Naming these in advance is how you protect against them.

Contribute to the Repository

Implementation reflections are among the most valuable contributions to the repository — they help future participants anticipate obstacles before they encounter them.
