Theoretical Foundation

What Digital Rhetoric Is — and Why It Matters for Teaching

Your students have never known a world without the internet. They consume digital media constantly, move between platforms fluidly, and produce digital content habitually. And most of them have never been taught to think rhetorically about any of it — to ask why a text takes the form it does, how platform shapes message, or what it means to write for a networked public rather than a single reader.

Digital rhetoric extends classical rhetorical theory — audience, purpose, ethos, pathos, logos — to account for the distinctive features of digital environments: hypertext, networked distribution, algorithmic curation, multimodality, and the collapse of the distinction between producer and consumer of content. It is not a new discipline but a new application of an ancient one.

Four features make digital environments rhetorically distinctive from print:

Multimodality

Digital texts combine written language, images, audio, data, and interactive elements. Each mode carries distinct rhetorical affordances; their relationships are themselves rhetorical choices.

Networked Distribution

Digital texts circulate, are shared, remixed, and stripped of context in ways print cannot. The implied audience is never only the intended reader.

Platform Architecture

A platform's character limits, recommendation algorithms, visual hierarchies, and monetization models are rhetorical systems that shape what gets communicated and how.

The fourth feature — the collapse of producer/consumer distinction — shapes what we need to teach: students are simultaneously audiences and authors of digital texts. Comfort with platforms does not translate into rhetorical awareness of them. That is what explicit instruction addresses.

Core Pedagogical Implication

Your job is not to teach students to use platforms — they know how. Your job is to teach them to analyze the rhetorical choices embedded in what they already do, and to make those choices intentionally in disciplinary and professional contexts.

Key Concepts

Platform Affordances, Hypertext, and Disciplinary Digital Communication

A platform affordance is what a platform makes easy, difficult, rewarded, or impossible. Twitter's (now X's) character limits reward provocation over nuance. YouTube's recommendation algorithm amplifies engagement over accuracy. Instagram's visual economy shapes which bodies, lifestyles, and aesthetics circulate as normal. These are not neutral features — they are rhetorical systems embedded in the platform's design and business model.

Hypertext changes the rhetorical situation in underestimated ways. A hyperlinked claim is a different rhetorical act than the same claim without a link. The link promises verification; it constructs a relationship between text and external authority; it invites readers to check, which changes how the claim functions rhetorically even for those who don't follow it.

Disciplinary digital communication varies considerably across fields. How scientists use preprint servers, how historians maintain public digital archives, how engineers document projects on professional platforms — these are discipline-specific rhetorical practices increasingly central to professional life. Part of digital communication instruction is helping students understand what disciplinary digital communication looks like in their field.

The Platform Critique Question

Teaching platform fluency alongside platform critique means asking: what does this platform make easy or rewarded, and whose communicative practices does that design privilege? Students who can answer this question are students who can resist rather than simply reproduce the rhetorical defaults of digital environments.

AI Systems as Platform Actors — A Preview of Module 3

Algorithmic recommendation systems, large language models, and AI-powered content moderation tools are now embedded in the platforms where digital communication happens. They are not neutral conduits — they are rhetorical actors that shape what circulates, what counts as credible, and whose communicative practices are amplified or suppressed. Analyzing a platform's affordances now requires identifying which of those affordances are produced by AI systems, and what that changes about who the system serves. Module 3 of this certificate gives you the full analytical framework for that analysis. For now, carry this question forward: when you describe a platform's rhetorical effects, which of those effects is an AI system producing?

Activity 1

Rhetorical Analysis of a Digital Text

Individual analysis · 60 minutes · 350–450 words

Select a digital text from your disciplinary or professional context — a text you might plausibly assign to students or that students in your field will encounter. Analyze it as a rhetorician, not just a content reader. Options include: a discipline-relevant website, a data visualization with narrative framing, a public-facing research summary, a professional organization's digital publication, or a science communication piece.

Your analysis should examine the text across five dimensions: the rhetorical situation (author, audience, purpose — stated vs. performed), platform affordances (how the platform shapes what the text can do), modal choices (how modes work together or in tension), rhetorical appeals (how the text establishes credibility, makes emotional appeals, deploys evidence), and pedagogical implications (what students would need to know to produce something like this).

Part A — Text Identification and Rhetorical Situation
What is the text, and what is its rhetorical situation?
Part B — Platform and Modal Analysis
How do platform and mode shape what this text can do?

Digital Communication in Your Discipline

Individual reflection · 45 minutes · 250–300 words

Every discipline has developed its own digital communication practices. This activity asks you to map those practices in your field and consider what students currently know about them — or don't. Resources

Module 1 Readings

  • ·Carter, Genesea M., and Aurora Matzke. "The More Digital Technology, the Better." In Bad Ideas About Writing, ed. Ball and Loewe. WVU Libraries (open access). A critical examination of uncritical techno-enthusiasm in education; establishes why more technology does not equal more learning.
  • ·Leverenz, Carrie S. "Design Thinking and the Wicked Problem of Teaching Writing." Computers and Composition, 33 (2014). Introduces design thinking as a framework for digital communication; treats digital composition as a design problem requiring iteration.
  • ·Daniel-Wariya, Joshua. "Play Is Not the Opposite of Work." In Bad Ideas About Writing. WVU Libraries (open access). Explores procedural rhetoric and the rhetorical dimensions of digital environments.
  • ·Writing Spaces, Vol. 1–3. Parlor Press (open access, writingspaces.org). Chapters on digital rhetoric, networked writing, and multimodal communication — searchable by topic.
  • ·CWSP Resource: Promoting Metacognitive Thought. Applicable to designing digital assignments with reflective components that make rhetorical decision-making visible.
