Discipline-Specific Writing Instruction

This module covers how a discipline's writing conventions carry its ways of knowing, how genre theory helps you name those conventions for students, and how the DAPOE framework (Gogan et al. 2022) helps you write assignment prompts that state what students need to know. It closes with the certificate capstone. Plan on 4 to 6 hours for the module and 4 to 6 more for the capstone.

Section 1

Ways of Knowing, Doing, and Writing

Michael Carter (2007) showed that disciplinary writing conventions express how a discipline produces, validates, and communicates knowledge. The lab report, the case brief, the ethnographic field note, and the philosophical argument each do different work. Each carries a theory of how evidence works, who counts as an authority, what counts as sound reasoning, and which claims a writer in that field is able to make.

If you want students to write well in your discipline, name these conventions for them. Practitioners learn their field's writing norms over years, and they stop noticing them. Ask yourself what makes a strong argument in your field. The answer often contains assumptions you hold and have never put into words, and students will not infer them from model texts alone.

A useful starting question for planning: what do writers in my discipline do? Consider what questions they ask, what evidence they gather, and how they position a claim against prior work. Then ask how you will make that work visible and teachable. Framed this way, writing instruction becomes an apprenticeship in the discipline.

Section 2

Genre as Social Action

In a 1984 essay, Carolyn Miller defined genre as social action: a typified response to a recurring social situation. Her definition moves attention from the formal features of a text to the purposes it serves. Writing teachers have built on it ever since. A research article takes its shape from the work it does. It stakes a claim, positions that claim against prior work, presents evidence that meets the field's standards, and invites critique from peers who share those standards.

Genre theory gives you better questions to ask of your own writing and your students'. What is this document trying to do? Whom does it address? Does it do that work well? Students carry these questions from one assignment, course, and discipline to the next, which a format checklist does not allow.

Genre knowledge travels

A student who knows what a genre is for (who knows, for instance, that a literature review positions a study within prior work) has a way to analyze an unfamiliar genre and work out what readers expect from it. A student who knows genre only as format reproduces familiar patterns and struggles when the task is new.

Section 3

The DAPOE Framework

Gogan, Singleterry, Caulfield, and Mallamo (2022) treat the assignment prompt as a genre with five essential components: directions, audience, purpose, objectives, and evaluation. They built DAPOE from four decades of research on assignment prompts, and they use it two ways. Instructors write prompts with it. Researchers read prompts with it to see which components a course or program leaves unstated.

D
Directions
The task itself and its specifications: what to do, how long, and when it is due.
A
Audience
The intended readers of the finished writing, named so students write past the instructor.
P
Purpose
What the writing does, and the disciplinary or professional work it participates in.
O
Objectives
The learning the assignment should produce, tied to course goals.
E
Evaluation
The criteria for grading, stated on the prompt.

Read as a diagnostic, the framework explains why some assignments produce weak writing. A prompt with clear directions and no named audience leaves students writing to the instructor, the reader they know. A prompt without stated objectives gives students no way to judge which parts of the task matter most. Each missing component produces its own pattern of problems, so the analysis points to the revision.

Using DAPOE in feedback

The same five terms give you language for response. In place of "your argument is weak," a comment can return the student to the prompt: "The assignment asks you to write for city council members. What would they need from your second section that a classmate would not?" Evaluation criteria stated on the prompt give writer and reader a shared vocabulary, which Gogan and colleagues, following Formo and Neary (2020), identify as one of the component's main benefits.

Section 4

Scaffolding Complex Writing Tasks

To scaffold a writing task, you break it into a sequence of smaller components. Writing teachers rely on this practice widely. A proposal comes before the research paper, an annotated bibliography before the literature review, a draft introduction before the full document. Each step gives students a finished piece of the larger task to build on.

Scaffolding does several jobs at once. It spreads the cognitive load across weeks, so students do not face a blank page for a high-stakes assignment with no prior work on the topic. It creates checkpoints where students receive feedback before the stakes are highest. It makes the process of disciplinary writing visible, and that visibility teaches. It also keeps students working on the assignment across the term. Most faculty suspect the alternative is a 48-hour push before the deadline.

Build each scaffold from the logic of the assignment itself. In a well-scaffolded assignment, every component does real work. The proposal requires a research question. The annotated bibliography requires students to read and assess sources. The draft introduction requires an argument. Components that exist only to enforce a timeline add work for everyone and teach little.

Section 5

Teaching Genre Awareness

Genre awareness is the metacognitive capacity to analyze the genre demands of a new writing situation and adapt to them. Students use it in every course that follows yours. Teach students to read genres analytically, to understand what each is designed to do, and to make informed choices about when to meet a genre's expectations and when to depart from them with purpose. Producing one specific genre well is a narrower goal.

Start by giving students strong examples to analyze before they write. Many faculty assign a genre without showing students what a good instance looks like. Analysis questions build the reading skills that writing in a genre depends on. How does this document introduce its argument? Whom does the author assume is reading? What kinds of evidence appear, and which are absent?

Backward design from model texts works well here. Collect three strong examples of the genre you assign, such as three research papers, three lab reports, or three policy briefs, and ask students to work out what makes them succeed. What do all three do? What do the strong examples do that weaker ones skip? When the class arrives at criteria together, students understand them because they built them.

Activity 1

Genre Analysis in Your Discipline

Individual research and analysis · 45–60 minutes

Choose one genre central to writing in your discipline: the research article, the case brief, the clinical note, the proposal, the technical report, the policy brief, or another form. Analyze it with the questions below.

Contribute to the repository

Genre analyses from specific disciplines help colleagues in other fields see how writing works across the curriculum. They also give students a model for analyzing unfamiliar genres.

Activity 2

Assignment Reverse-Engineering

Critical analysis · 45–60 minutes

Choose one writing assignment from your course, ideally one you have used before and whose results you have seen. Use the DAPOE framework to diagnose what the assignment asks students to do and where it confuses them or invites weak writing.

Contribute to the repository

A revised assignment with a short rationale shows other faculty what changed and why. Your revised prompt with DAPOE annotations will help colleagues who are designing similar assignments.

Activity 3

DAPOE Assignment Design Workshop

Design activity · 60–90 minutes · Forms the foundation of your capstone

Design a new writing assignment with the DAPOE framework. Make it specific to your discipline, draw on your genre analysis from Activity 1, and build in scaffolding. This assignment becomes the center of your capstone.

Contribute to the repository

A complete DAPOE assignment, especially from a STEM or professional discipline, gives colleagues a model to adapt. Share the full assignment, or share the design framework with a note on your course context.

Certificate Capstone

Written Communication Capstone Submission

Your capstone is a writing-intensive syllabus redesign or curriculum plan that applies the frameworks from all three modules. Build it for use: a syllabus you will teach from, or a curriculum plan you intend to develop.

Part A. Course description and writing context

Describe the course (or curriculum) you are redesigning. Explain the writing demands of the course as it stands and where students tend to struggle. Draw on Module 1 here: transfer, metacognition, and the relationship between process and product.

Part B. Redesigned assignment sequence

Present at least one major writing assignment redesigned with the DAPOE framework, along with its scaffolding sequence. Start from your Activity 3 assignment and revise it. If you are redesigning a whole course, include 2–3 assignments and show how they build on each other.

Part C. Feedback and assessment plan

Describe how you will respond to student writing in this course. Which Module 2 strategies (minimal marking, screencast feedback, peer review protocols, revised rubrics) will you use, and how? Include any rubric or feedback protocol you have designed.

Part D. Reflective rationale

In 1–2 pages, explain the thinking behind your redesign. Which frameworks from the three modules helped most? What surprised you? What do you expect to be hardest to put into practice? Write in your own voice, as someone who now thinks about writing as a subject.
