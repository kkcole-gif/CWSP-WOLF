Discipline-Specific Writing Instruction

How writing conventions are embedded in disciplinary ways of knowing, how genre theory gives faculty a framework for making those conventions explicit, and how the DAPOE framework structures writing assignments that develop disciplinary thinking. Includes the certificate capstone submission. Estimated time: 4–6 hours plus capstone.

Section 1

Ways of Knowing, Doing, and Writing

Michael Carter's research (2007) demonstrated that disciplinary writing conventions are not arbitrary stylistic preferences — they are expressions of how disciplines produce, validate, and communicate knowledge. The lab report, the case brief, the ethnographic field note, and the philosophical argument are not interchangeable genres. Each encodes a theory of how evidence works, who counts as an authoritative source, what counts as valid reasoning, and what kind of claim is sayable in that field.

This has a significant implication for writing instruction: faculty who want students to write well in their disciplines need to make these epistemic conventions explicit. Most disciplinary writing norms are invisible to their practitioners precisely because they are so deeply internalized. Asking "what makes a strong argument in your field?" often surfaces assumptions that faculty hold but have never articulated — assumptions that students cannot possibly infer from reading exemplary texts alone.

The productive question is not "how do I teach students to write?" but "what do writers in my discipline actually do — what questions do they ask, what kinds of evidence do they marshal, how do they position their claims relative to prior work — and how do I make that process visible and teachable?" This reframes writing instruction as disciplinary apprenticeship rather than skills training.

Section 2

Genre as Social Action

Carolyn Miller's foundational 1984 essay defined genre not as a text type but as a social action — a typified response to a recurring social situation. This definition has been enormously productive for writing instruction because it shifts attention from the formal features of texts to the social purposes they serve. A research article doesn't look the way it does because of stylistic convention; it looks that way because it is doing something specific — staking a claim, positioning it relative to prior work, presenting evidence that meets the field's standards, and inviting critique from peers who share those standards.

Genre theory helps faculty ask better questions about their own writing and their students' writing. Not "does this meet the format requirements?" but "what is this document trying to do, who is it addressing, and is it doing that thing effectively?" These are questions that transfer across assignments, courses, and disciplines in a way that format checklists do not.

Genre knowledge as transferable

Students who understand what genres are for — who understand that a literature review positions a study, not merely summarizes prior work — can analyze new genres they encounter and understand what is expected of them. Students who understand genre only as format can reproduce familiar patterns but struggle with unfamiliar writing tasks.

Section 3

The DAPOE Framework

The DAPOE framework provides a practical structure for designing and evaluating writing assignments across disciplines. Each step represents a phase of the writing process and a set of questions instructors can build into assignments, class discussion, or feedback prompts.

D
Discover
What is already known? What is the current conversation?
A
Ask
What specific question does this assignment prompt the writer to answer?
P
Produce
What genre or form is appropriate for this question and audience?
O
Own
What is the writer's distinctive contribution or position?
E
Evaluate
By what criteria will the work be judged? What counts as success?

The framework is most useful as a design tool — for creating assignments that are explicit about each step — and as a diagnostic tool — for analyzing why existing assignments aren't producing the writing you want. An assignment that goes wrong at the "Discover" phase (students don't understand what the existing conversation is) will produce different problems than one that goes wrong at "Own" (students summarize rather than stake a position).

Using DAPOE in feedback

DAPOE also provides vocabulary for targeted feedback. Rather than "your argument is weak," you can say: "You've done well at Discovering the existing research, but your Ask — the specific question your paper addresses — isn't yet clear. What exactly are you claiming that the sources you've read don't already tell us?" This makes feedback actionable.

Section 4

Scaffolding Complex Writing Tasks

Scaffolding — breaking a complex writing task into sequenced components — is one of the most consistently supported practices in writing pedagogy. Students who complete a proposal before a research paper, who submit an annotated bibliography before a literature review, who share a draft introduction before writing the full document, produce substantially better final products than students given the same task without scaffolding.

Scaffolding serves several functions simultaneously. It distributes cognitive load across time, preventing the paralysis that comes from facing a blank page for a high-stakes assignment with no prior engagement with the topic. It creates checkpoints at which students can receive feedback before the stakes are highest. It makes the process of disciplinary writing visible, which is itself a form of instruction. And it motivates early and sustained engagement with the assignment rather than the 48-hour marathon that most faculty assume is happening.

The most effective scaffolding is integrated into the logic of the assignment rather than added as external busywork. A scaffolded assignment has components that each do real work — the proposal forces a research question, the annotated bibliography forces engagement with sources, the draft introduction forces an argument — rather than checkboxes that exist primarily to enforce timelines.

Section 5

Teaching Genre Awareness

Genre awareness — the metacognitive capacity to analyze and adapt to the genre demands of new writing situations — is among the most valuable and transferable writing skills a student can develop. The goal is not to teach students to produce a specific genre but to teach them to read genres analytically, understand what they are designed to do, and make informed choices about how to meet genre expectations or depart from them productively.

Teaching genre awareness requires giving students exemplary texts to analyze before they write. This sounds obvious, but in practice many faculty assign genres without showing students what good instances of those genres look like. Analysis prompts — "how does this document introduce its argument?", "who does the author seem to assume is reading this?", "what kinds of evidence appear, and what kinds are absent?" — develop the genre-reading skills that underlie genre-writing skills.

Backward design from exemplary texts is particularly effective. Take three strong examples of the genre you assign — three excellent research papers, three effective lab reports, three compelling policy briefs — and give students the task of reverse-engineering what makes them work. What do all three do? What do strong examples do that weak ones don't? This analysis, collectively arrived at, produces criteria that students understand because they derived them, not criteria they received as mandates.

Activity 1

Genre Analysis in Your Discipline

Individual research and analysis · 45–60 minutes

Select one genre that is central to writing in your discipline — the research article, the case brief, the clinical note, the proposal, the technical report, the policy brief, or another form. Analyze it using the questions below.

Contribute to the repository

Genre analyses from specific disciplines are among the most useful cross-disciplinary resources — they help faculty understand how writing works across the curriculum and give students a framework for analyzing unfamiliar genres.

Activity 2

Assignment Reverse-Engineering

Critical analysis · 45–60 minutes

Select one writing assignment from your course — ideally one you've used before and seen the results of. Apply the DAPOE framework to diagnose what the assignment is asking students to do and where it may create confusion or produce weak writing.

Contribute to the repository

Revised assignments — especially when accompanied by a rationale explaining what was changed and why — are among the most requested items in the repository. Your revised assignment prompt with DAPOE annotations would be directly useful to colleagues designing similar assignments.

Activity 3

DAPOE Assignment Design Workshop

Design activity · 60–90 minutes · Forms the foundation of your capstone

Design a new writing assignment from scratch using the DAPOE framework. This assignment should be discipline-specific, reflect the genre analysis you completed in Activity 1, and incorporate scaffolding. It will become the centerpiece of your capstone submission.

Contribute to the repository

A fully DAPOE-designed assignment, especially in a STEM or professional discipline, would be among the most valuable additions to the repository. You can share the full assignment or just the design framework with your context.

Written Communication Capstone Submission

Your capstone is a writing-intensive syllabus redesign or curriculum plan that demonstrates your application of the three modules' frameworks. It should be a real document — a syllabus you will actually use, or a curriculum plan you intend to develop — not a hypothetical exercise.

Part A — Course description and writing context

Describe the course (or curriculum) you are redesigning. Explain the writing demands of the course as it currently exists and what students typically struggle with. This section should demonstrate your Module 1 thinking — transfer, metacognition, and the relationship between process and product.

Part B — Redesigned assignment sequence

Present at least one major writing assignment redesigned using the DAPOE framework, with a scaffolding sequence. This should be your Activity 3 assignment, revised and refined. If you are redesigning a course, include 2–3 assignments showing how they build on each other.

Part C — Feedback and assessment plan

Describe how you will respond to student writing in this course. Which of the Module 2 strategies — minimal marking, screencast feedback, peer review protocols, revised rubrics — will you incorporate, and how? Include any rubric or feedback protocol you have designed.

Part D — Reflective rationale

A 1–2 page reflective rationale explaining the thinking behind your redesign. Which frameworks from the three modules were most useful? What surprised you? What do you expect to be most challenging to implement? This section should demonstrate your voice as a writing-about-writing thinker.
