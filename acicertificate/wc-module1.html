Foundations of Writing Pedagogy

This module covers why writing knowledge stays tied to the context where students learned it, how reflection helps them carry it forward, and what decades of research on low-stakes writing and the writing process mean for your assignments. Plan on 4 to 6 hours over two weeks.

What this module asks you to do

  1. Explain how writing transfer and metacognition shape student writing in a disciplinary course. (Sections 1 and 2, Activity 3)
  2. Evaluate your assumptions about student writing against research on writing development. (Activity 1)
  3. Distinguish the writing skills your course must teach explicitly from those students bring from earlier courses. (Activity 3)
  4. Distinguish process-oriented from product-oriented writing assignments in your own course. (Activity 1, prompt 5)
  5. Design three low-stakes writing activities tied to your course goals. (Activity 2)
  6. Use one low-stakes activity in a course and report what students did with it. (Activity 2, your commitment, and a short follow-up from CWSP near the end of the term)
Section 1

Writing Transfer

Many writing curricula rest on an assumption: a student who learns to write well in one course will write well in the next. Research on transfer shows otherwise. What students learn about writing in high school, in first-year composition, or in another department's courses does not arrive intact in yours.

You have probably seen the result. A student who wrote capable essays in first-year writing hands in a lab report or a case analysis that misses the mark. The student has not forgotten how to write. What counts as evidence, how an argument is built, and which tone fits the reader all change from one discipline to another, and the student is meeting your discipline's version for the first time.

Writing knowledge does not move on its own from one course to the next. Ellen Carillo makes the case in a short essay for Bad Ideas About Writing (Ball and Loewe 2017), an edited collection in which writing studies scholars answer common beliefs about writing. Carillo observes that research on teaching for transfer, whatever its method, converges on one recommendation: build metacognitive exercises into writing courses. The empirical studies behind her claim come from Beaufort (2007), who followed one writer from first-year composition into the workplace, and from Yancey, Robertson, and Taczak (2014), who tested the claim in the classroom. Students whose instructors taught explicitly for transfer carried their writing knowledge into new contexts more regularly than students in other writing courses. Their curriculum asked students to build a theory of writing, which they carried into each new situation.

Practical implications

Name the conventions you expect, and explain why your field writes this way. Students who understand the reasons behind a convention have something to take into the next course. Watch, too, for habits students bring with them. The five-paragraph essay, for instance, teaches a structure many disciplines do not use.

Section 2

The Role of Metacognition

Metacognition, thinking about one's own thinking, is how writing knowledge travels. Carillo's review and the Yancey, Robertson, and Taczak study point the same direction. Students who understand what they are doing when they write, and why a strategy suits a situation, adapt to new writing tasks more readily than students who practice without reflecting.

A metacognitive writer explains why she made a choice, notices when a familiar approach does not fit, and checks her own understanding while drafting. None of this develops by exposure. It comes from explicit instruction, structured reflection, and practice in unfamiliar situations.

In practice, ask students to write about their writing. A short reflection attached to a major assignment, asking what choices they made, what surprised them, and what they would change, builds the habit. Low-stakes reflective writing placed beside high-stakes work is one of the most efficient ways to do it.

Section 3

Low-Stakes and High-Stakes Writing

Peter Elbow's distinction between low-stakes and high-stakes writing (1997) has shaped writing across the curriculum for nearly three decades. High-stakes writing, such as the formal essay, the lab report, or the research paper, asks students to demonstrate what they know. Low-stakes writing, such as the entrance ticket, the weekly reading response, or the reflective journal, asks them to think on the page while little rides on the result.

Low-stakes writing does several jobs at once. Frequent writing builds fluency, even when the writing is rough. A five-minute response at the start of class shows you what students understood from the reading and where they lost the thread. Exploratory writing generates material students later develop in formal assignments. Students who write often for low stakes also come to see writing as a way of thinking, which eases the pressure many feel when every page is graded.

Respond to low-stakes writing without grading it. A completion mark, a check, or a remark at the start of the next class ("Several of you wrote about the second case; let's start there") keeps students accountable and keeps your workload manageable. That economy is what lets the practice last a whole semester.

Low stakes, high demand

Low stakes describes the consequences for the student. The task itself is often demanding. A reading response might require careful analysis, and an entrance ticket might ask for a claim. The low stakes let students take risks, try claims they are unsure of, and think in front of you without paying for it in the gradebook.

Section 4

Process and Product

Since the 1970s, writing teachers have debated where instruction belongs: in the process of writing (prewriting, drafting, revising, editing) or in the qualities of the finished product. The process movement arose as a corrective to teaching that graded finished papers without attention to how students produced them.

Writing is recursive. Writers circle back, find out what they think by drafting, revise at several levels at once, and often learn where an argument is going only near the end. Courses built around this, with time for more than one draft and revision as the center of the work, give students practice in the part of writing where most of the thinking happens.

Standards for the finished product still matter. Feedback on finished products evaluates. Feedback on drafts teaches students to use feedback. Proposals, annotated bibliographies, peer review, and revision memos make the work of writing visible, which makes it teachable. Final papers improve as a result.

Activity 1

Writing Pedagogy Assumptions Audit

Individual reflective writing · 30–45 minutes · Reflective journal format

Everyone who assigns writing works from beliefs about it, most of them formed long before any reading in writing studies. This activity asks you to write those beliefs down and test them against the research in this module.

Give each prompt at least a paragraph. Describe what you do now in your courses. Nobody grades this.

What a complete response does

  • Names specific conventions, genres, or skills you assume students bring.
  • Traces your standard for good writing to at least one source.
  • Points to features of an assignment sheet showing whether it is process- or product-oriented.
  • Names one tension between your assumptions and the research in Sections 1 to 3.

Prompts marked * are required to submit the module.

Share with the repository

Your audit stays private unless you choose otherwise. If you are willing to share its themes (not your full text), CWSP will use them to see which assumptions recur across disciplines and to revise this module.

Activity 2

Low-Stakes Writing Design Workshop

Collaborative design activity · 45–60 minutes

Design three low-stakes writing activities for a course you teach. For each, give the learning goal, the time it takes, how you will respond, and how it connects to the larger goals of the course.

What a complete response does

  • Writes each activity as the prompt students will see.
  • Ties each activity to a goal of the course.
  • States the time each activity takes and how you will respond to it.
  • Names the course and the week where you will first use one activity.

Prompts marked * are required to submit the module.

Near the end of the term, CWSP will email you one question about what students did with the activity. Your answer completes objective 6.

Contribute to the repository

Faculty in other departments adapt low-stakes activities more readily than almost anything else in the repository. If you share yours, CWSP will review them and credit you.

Activity 3

Transfer Mapping Exercise

Individual analysis · 30 minutes

Map the writing knowledge your course requires against what students bring from earlier courses and experience. The gaps show you what to teach explicitly.

What a complete response does

  • Lists three to five writing skills specific enough to teach.
  • Names where students met each skill before, if anywhere.
  • Separates the skills your course must teach from those students bring.
  • Names at least one habit from prior learning likely to interfere, and one change you will make.

Prompts marked * are required to submit the module.

Contribute to the repository

Transfer maps help most when they cross departments. A map from an engineering course shows a history instructor what her students learned, and mislearned, in their STEM courses.
