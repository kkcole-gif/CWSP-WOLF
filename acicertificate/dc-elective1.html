Digital Communication Certificate · Elective 1

Social Media and the Attention Economy in Education

This elective covers how social media platforms design for engagement, what their AI recommendation systems reproduce, and how you teach students to analyze the platforms they use every day and to use them with critical awareness. Plan on 3 to 4 hours.

Section 1

The Attention Economy

When a student's attention drifts from a reading to a phone, the student is responding to a system built to pull it there. Platform companies design their products, often with teams of behavioral scientists and machine learning researchers, to capture and hold attention. Notification timing, variable rewards, infinite scroll, and personalized feeds are deliberate design choices. Each is tuned to raise engagement metrics, and those metrics compete by design with sustained concentration, careful thinking, and deliberate reflection.

In the attention economy, attention is the commodity. Social media companies charge users nothing because they sell user attention and behavioral data to advertisers. Their designs aim to increase time on the platform and the data collected, and those aims often conflict with users' epistemic wellbeing. Moral outrage and novelty travel far on these platforms. Brady et al. (2017) found that each moral-emotional word in a message about a polarizing issue raised its diffusion by about 20 percent. Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral (2018) found that false news on Twitter spread farther and faster than true news, and that its novelty helps explain why people shared it.

What this means for your course

When you ask students for slow, deliberate thinking, you ask for the mode of attention these platforms are built to interrupt. Knowing this is the starting point for bringing social media into a course with enough critical awareness to teach students to use it well.

Section 2

Algorithmic Recommendation Systems as AI

The recommendation systems on social media platforms are AI systems. They use machine learning to predict which content a given user is most likely to engage with. Apply the AI literacy framework (Cole, 2026) to them and their structural dimensions come into view.

At the conceptual level, a recommendation system curates according to its training. It learns from engagement data (clicks, watch time, shares, comments) and is optimized to produce more of the behavior that generates that data. Accuracy, reliable sourcing, and relevance to a student's education sit outside that objective. Its outputs reflect the objective it has: engagement.

At the structural level, these systems reproduce the hierarchies encoded in their data, the pattern Noble (2018) documents in search algorithms. A system trained to maximize engagement learns to favor the content Brady et al. and Vosoughi et al. describe, so measured, qualified voices reach fewer people than inflammatory ones.

At the operative level, bringing social media into a course calls for a decision grounded in this structural analysis. What does it mean to require students to open accounts on platforms whose recommendation systems optimize for engagement at the expense of their epistemic wellbeing? What accountability conditions would make that requirement ethically defensible?

Section 3

Platform Literacy and Platform Fluency

Platform fluency means knowing how to use a platform's features well. Platform literacy means understanding what the platform does to the communication that passes through it. A student might be highly fluent in TikTok production (transitions, audio syncing, trending sounds, hashtag strategy) and still have no analytical account of how the algorithm distributes content, what data the platform collects, or how its design sets certain communicative norms as the default.

A platform-literate student sees the platform itself as a rhetorical and structural actor. It shapes what communication is possible, what gets amplified, who gets heard, and at what cost. Teaching social media literacy means teaching students to ask structural questions. What kind of AI system is this recommendation engine? What does it optimize for, and what does that optimization reproduce? Who built it, and whose interests does the design serve?

A course that brings in social media without platform literacy teaches students to use a tool while leaving its structural effects unexamined. This elective gives you enough platform literacy to teach it and to design assignments that build it in students.

Section 4

Social Media in Your Discipline

Every discipline has some relationship to social media, as a site of public communication, a source of misinformation about the field's findings, a channel for researchers, or a professional expectation. Scientists use Twitter/X to share findings with public audiences. Public health researchers track misinformation on social platforms. Journalists produce content for social distribution. Educators build professional learning communities on LinkedIn or Bluesky. Start with how your discipline uses social media. Assignments built from those uses develop the specific social media literacy your field requires.

Activity 1

Rhetorical Analysis of Social Media Content

Individual analysis · 45–60 minutes

Choose one piece of social media content from your discipline: a viral post about a scientific finding, a researcher's thread, a TikTok explanation of a disciplinary concept, a professional organization's account, or a widely shared infographic. Analyze it as a rhetorical artifact and as a product of the platform's algorithmic system.

Contribute to the repository

Analyses from your discipline, especially those that show how algorithmic amplification affects disciplinary content, help build a cross-disciplinary resource for teaching platform literacy.

Activity 2

Social Media Platform Decision

Structural analysis + operative decision · 60–75 minutes

Choose one social media platform you use or are considering for your course, whether for student communication, assignment submission, professional community, or content analysis. Apply the AI literacy framework to its recommendation system. Then make an operative decision about whether and how to use it.

Key readings and resources

  • ★ Carter, G. M., & Matzke, A. (2017). The more digital technology, the better. In Bad Ideas About Writing. WVU Libraries. Foundational critique of uncritical technology adoption, the analytical basis for evaluating social media use in education.
  • Daniel-Wariya, J. (2017). Gamification makes writing fun. In C. E. Ball & D. M. Loewe (Eds.), Bad Ideas About Writing. WVU Libraries. A critique of gamified design, relevant to how platforms build engagement into their structure.
  • Brady, W. J., Wills, J. A., Jost, J. T., Tucker, J. A., & Van Bavel, J. J. (2017). Emotion shapes the diffusion of moralized content in social networks. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 114(28), 7313–7318.
  • Noble, S. U. (2018). Algorithms of oppression. NYU Press, Chapters 1–2. Structural analysis of algorithmic systems and the hierarchies they reproduce, the methodological model for Activity 2.
  • Vosoughi, S., Roy, D., & Aral, S. (2018). The spread of true and false news online. Science, 359(6380), 1146–1151.
  • Writing Spaces, Vol. 3 (writingspaces.org). Chapters on social media, digital audiences, and algorithmic writing environments.
