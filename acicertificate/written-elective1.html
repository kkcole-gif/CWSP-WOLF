Multimodal and Digital Writing

An introduction to multimodal composition as a rhetorical practice in your discipline — and a clear path forward for those who want the full analytical and pedagogical framework.

Framework

Multimodality Is Not a Technology Question

Multimodal composition — writing that combines written language with images, data visualization, audio, video, or interactive elements — is often framed as a matter of tools and platforms. The real question is rhetorical: what does each mode contribute that the others cannot, and how do their relationships shape meaning?

Every mode carries distinct affordances. Written language excels at precise argumentation and qualification. Images communicate holistically and quickly, but are harder to make precise. Data visualization reveals patterns in ways neither prose nor images alone can. Audio conveys tone, urgency, and identity in ways text cannot replicate. Multimodal texts work when these modes complement and extend each other; they fail when they simply repeat the same content in different forms.

For teaching purposes, the key principle is this: multimodal composition is a rhetorical practice before it is a technical one. Students who understand why each mode is doing what it is doing will produce more intentional multimodal texts than students who are simply told to "add visuals" or "make it engaging."

The Non-Negotiable: Reflection

Any multimodal writing assignment should include a reflective component that asks students to explain their modal choices — why this mode for this content, for this audience, toward this purpose. The reflection is not a bonus; it is where rhetorical learning consolidates. Without it, multimodal assignments can produce technically competent texts that students cannot explain or repeat intentionally.

AI and Multimodal Generation — A Note

AI image generators, text-to-video tools, and automated data visualization now make multimodal production technically accessible in ways that were not possible even recently. This makes the rhetorical question more important, not less: when any mode can be generated on demand, the question of why this mode, making this choice, for this audience becomes the intellectual core of the assignment. An assignment that can be completed by generating an AI image without any rhetorical deliberation has not specified its rhetorical situation clearly enough. Activity 1

Analyzing a Multimodal Text in Your Discipline

Individual analysis · 45 minutes · 250–350 words

Find a multimodal text from your disciplinary context — something students in your field might produce, encounter professionally, or be asked to read. Analyze it across four lenses: what the text is trying to do rhetorically, how the modes work together, what disciplinary conventions it follows, and what a student would need to know to produce something like it.

Good candidates include: a research poster, a data-driven report with visualizations, a science communication piece that combines text and images, an engineering design document, a public-facing video summary of research, or a social media campaign with multiple connected posts.
