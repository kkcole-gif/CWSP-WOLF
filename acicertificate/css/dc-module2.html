Designing Digital Communication Assignments

Principles for creating effective digital assignments, strategies for tool selection that serves learning rather than novelty, and accessibility requirements that apply to all digital communication in educational contexts. Estimated time: 3–5 hours over two weeks.

Section 1

Principles for Digital Assignment Design

The same frameworks that make print writing assignments effective — clear genre, explicit audience, scaffolded process, transparent criteria — apply to digital assignments. But digital assignments carry additional design considerations: tool friction (what does it cost students in time and money to acquire the technical skill needed?), platform constraints (what does the platform allow and not allow?), and persistence (who owns the work after the semester ends, and where does it live?).

Effective digital assignment design starts with the communication goal, not the tool. "I want students to make a podcast" is a tool choice before a pedagogical decision. "I want students to communicate their research findings to a general audience in an engaging, accessible format" is a communication goal that might lead to a podcast, a recorded presentation, a short video, an infographic, or a data visualization — depending on what serves the learning best and what students can realistically produce.

Genre clarity

Name the genre and provide examples. A "digital story" is not clear. A "3–5 minute documentary-style video with a voiced-over argument" is.

Explicit audience

Identify a real or realistic audience. "Post to the class discussion board" is not an audience. "Write for the parents of children with Type 1 diabetes" is.

Proportionate tool complexity

The tool should add to the communication, not dominate the learning. Students shouldn't spend most of their time fighting software.

Scaffolded process

Build in a draft, a prototype, or a peer review before the final submission. Digital projects have high failure rates without intermediate checkpoints.

Section 2

Tool Selection: Learning First

The proliferation of digital tools for academic communication creates a genuine design challenge: there are almost always multiple tools that could work for a given assignment, with different affordances, cost structures, learning curves, and accessibility profiles. The decision should be driven by what the tool enables communicatively, not by what is novel, what the instructor prefers, or what looks impressive on a portfolio.

Key questions for tool selection: Is this tool free or covered by NC State licensing? Does it run on the devices students actually have? Does it produce accessible output (captioned video, screen-reader-compatible documents, alt-text-enabled images)? Does it require creating accounts on third-party platforms, and if so, what are the privacy implications for student work? Is technical instruction time available, or will students be expected to figure it out independently?

NC State digital tools available to students

NC State provides students access to the Adobe Creative Cloud suite, Microsoft 365 (including Teams and Stream), Canvas media tools, and various discipline-specific software through NCSU Libraries. Designing assignments around these tools reduces access friction significantly. CWSP can help identify appropriate tools for specific assignment types — contact kkcole2@ncsu.edu.

When a tool has a significant learning curve, that learning is part of the assignment — and should be treated as such. Explicitly scaffolding the technical skill acquisition (a tutorial completion, a practice exercise, a low-stakes prototype) alongside the communication task is what makes the difference between an assignment that develops technical capacity and one that just frustrates students.

Section 3

Accessibility in Digital Communication

WCAG 2.1 (Web Content Accessibility Guidelines) defines the accessibility standards that apply to all digital content in educational contexts — and, increasingly, in professional contexts. Teaching students to produce accessible digital communication is not a compliance requirement layered on top of communication instruction; it is communication instruction. Accessible design requires genuine attention to audience — specifically, to the full range of audiences who might encounter and use a digital artifact.

Core accessibility requirements for student-produced digital work include: captions or transcripts for all audio and video content; alt text for all non-decorative images; sufficient color contrast between text and background; keyboard navigability for interactive content; and meaningful link text (not "click here").

Faculty who require accessible digital work need to teach it explicitly — most students have never encountered accessibility requirements and many have never read content with a screen reader or navigated a site without a mouse. A brief accessibility audit activity (seeing how their draft works with a free screen reader, running it through a contrast checker) is the most efficient way to build genuine understanding of what accessibility means in practice.

Section 4

Scaffolding Digital Projects

Digital projects have a particular failure mode that print assignments don't: technical catastrophe close to the deadline. Files become corrupted, platforms go down, students lose hours of work. Scaffolding digital projects with intermediate deliverables — a storyboard, a script, a rough cut, a prototype — reduces this risk and also produces better final work by making the production process visible and teachable.

Scaffolding a digital project effectively means sequencing the conceptual work before the technical work. Students should have a clear argument, a defined audience, and a planned structure before they open any software. A one-page planning document or a hand-drawn storyboard that describes what the digital artifact will argue, who it addresses, and what it will look like — before any production begins — dramatically improves the quality and efficiency of digital production.

Peer review for digital projects works differently than for written drafts. Peers need guiding questions that are specific to the digital genre: "Is the argument clear in the first 30 seconds?", "Can you navigate without reading?", "What's missing from the caption track?", "Does the color choice communicate the right tone for the audience?" These questions develop both reviewer and producer's understanding of the genre.

Section 5

Genre Conventions in Digital Communication

Each digital genre has its own conventions that readers and viewers have been trained to expect. A data visualization that violates the conventions of its genre — using 3D charts that distort proportions, omitting axes labels, using misleading scales — communicates inaccuracy or incompetence, regardless of whether the underlying data is correct. A research blog post that reads like a journal article, with dense paragraphs and no headers or visuals, fails the genre even if the content is strong.

Teaching genre conventions requires showing students examples — and annotating what makes exemplary examples work. Genre annotation activities, where students analyze three strong examples of a target genre and identify what they share, are highly effective for developing genre awareness before production begins. This approach also surfaces disciplinary variation: the conventions of a science communication blog post are different from those of a humanities public scholarship post, which differ from a professional LinkedIn article.

Activity 1

Digital Assignment Design Using DAPOE

Design activity · 60–90 minutes

Using the DAPOE framework from the Written Communication certificate (or the version below), design one digital communication assignment for your course. If you haven't completed WC Module 3, the DAPOE framework asks: Discover (what background?), Ask (what question?), Produce (what genre/format?), Own (what is the student's distinctive contribution?), Evaluate (by what criteria?).
