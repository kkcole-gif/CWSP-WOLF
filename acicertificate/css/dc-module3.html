Assessing Digital Communication

The distinctive challenges of evaluating multimodal and digital work, strategies for rubric design that account for what digital genres actually do, portfolio approaches for cumulative digital learning, and peer feedback protocols calibrated to digital production. Estimated time: 3–5 hours plus capstone.

Section 1

The Assessment Challenges of Digital Work

Digital communication assignments surface assessment challenges that print assignments don't face in the same way. Three stand out as most significant for faculty.

First, mode-specificity. A rubric designed for written argument doesn't transfer to video, podcast, or data visualization. The criteria that make a research paper strong — arguable thesis, evidence-to-claim ratio, citation practices — don't map directly onto what makes a 5-minute documentary strong. Faculty often apply print criteria to digital work and produce assessments that miss what the work is actually doing (or failing to do) as digital communication.

Second, process invisibility. With a print assignment, revision is visible — earlier drafts exist, tracked changes can be reviewed, and the distance between first draft and final is auditable. Digital production is often less transparent: a final video reveals little about how many takes it took, how the script changed, or what technical choices were made. Assessment that only evaluates the final product misses a significant portion of the learning.

Third, technical variation. Students in the same course working on the same digital assignment will have wildly different levels of technical fluency with the relevant tools. An assessment rubric that inadvertently rewards technical polish over communication quality will disadvantage students who spent time on the argument rather than the production values — and may reward the reverse.

The core principle

Assess the communication, not the production. A visually polished infographic that makes a weak argument or misrepresents data should score lower than a simply designed infographic with a clear, accurate, well-supported argument. Rubric criteria should be weighted to reflect this priority.

Section 2

Rubrics for Digital and Multimodal Work

Effective rubrics for digital work share the design principles of effective rubrics for any genre — they should describe what the communication does rather than what it fails to do, they should reflect the actual goals of the assignment, and they should be shared with students before they begin. But they also need criteria specific to digital communication.

A well-designed rubric for a digital communication assignment typically addresses three clusters of criteria: rhetorical effectiveness (does the artifact achieve its communicative purpose with its intended audience?); mode-specific quality (does it use the specific modes it engages — visual, audio, spatial, linguistic — effectively?); and accessibility and technical execution (is it accessible to the intended audience, including people with disabilities, and does it function as intended?).

The relative weight of these clusters should be explicit and justified. Rhetorical effectiveness should typically carry the most weight. Mode-specific quality rewards disciplinary genre knowledge. Accessibility and technical execution ensures students attend to the full communication context — but should not be weighted so heavily that a student who produces a technically flawless artifact with a weak argument receives a high score.

Holistic descriptors are particularly useful for digital genres: a paragraph that describes what an "excellent," "proficient," "developing," and "beginning" version of the artifact looks like — as a whole, not a sum of parts — often gives students clearer guidance than an analytic grid and gives faculty more useful interpretive flexibility.

Section 3

Portfolio Approaches for Digital Learning

Digital portfolios — curated collections of student work with reflective commentary — are particularly well suited to digital communication instruction because they can house the digital artifacts themselves, alongside process documentation (storyboards, drafts, peer feedback received) and reflective writing about what students learned. They make digital communication learning visible in a way that individual assignment grades cannot.

Effective digital portfolios are curated, not comprehensive. Students select artifacts, revise them for the portfolio context, and write reflective introductions that explain the selection rationale and articulate what was learned. The curation and reflection are where the deepest learning happens — and where faculty can assess metacognitive development alongside the quality of the artifacts.

Platform choice matters. GitHub Pages, WordPress (available through NC State), Google Sites, and Adobe Portfolio are all viable platforms with different strengths. The key is that the platform should serve the portfolio's audience and purpose, not be selected because it's familiar to the faculty member or impressive-looking. A portfolio on Google Sites that is easy to navigate and clearly organized will serve students better than a complex platform that students spent most of their time learning to operate.

Section 4

Peer Feedback Protocols for Digital Work

Peer feedback for digital work requires genre-specific protocols even more than peer review of writing does, because reviewers need explicit guidance about what to look for in a mode they may not be expert in. Without structure, peer feedback on a podcast tends to address voice quality and sound levels ("it was hard to hear you in some parts") rather than argument, audience, and genre conventions.

Structured peer review protocols for digital work give reviewers specific listening or viewing tasks: "Describe, in one sentence, the argument you heard. Was there a clear moment when the main claim was stated?" is a more useful prompt than "Was the podcast good?" Protocols that require reviewers to identify specific timestamps or specific visual elements — "At 2:14, the argument shifts — is this transition clear?" — produce more specific, more useful feedback.

Reviewer training is also important. Brief exposure to exemplary work in the genre, with discussion of what makes it effective, calibrates peer reviewers before they give feedback. This calibration activity doubles as genre instruction — students who have analyzed three strong data visualizations for what they share are much better positioned to give useful feedback on a peer's data visualization than students who have only seen the assignment prompt.

Activity 1

Multimodal Rubric Design

Design activity · 60–90 minutes

Design a rubric for the digital assignment you created in Module 2 Activity 1. Your rubric should address the three clusters described in Section 2: rhetorical effectiveness, mode-specific quality, and accessibility/technical execution. Make the weighting explicit and justified.

Contribute to the repository

A well-designed rubric for a specific digital genre — especially from a STEM or professional discipline — is one of the most useful contributions a faculty member can make to the repository.

Activity 2

Digital Portfolio Assessment Plan

Design activity · 45–60 minutes

Design a digital portfolio framework for one of your courses — either a portfolio that would replace or supplement a final exam, or a portfolio that documents students' digital communication development across a course or program. You don't have to implement this immediately; the goal is to think through what it would take.

Contribute to the repository

Portfolio assessment frameworks are particularly transferable across disciplines. A portfolio model designed for an engineering capstone course can adapt to a communication, public health, or social work context with minimal modification.

Digital Communication Capstone Submission

Your capstone is a digital assignment portfolio: a curated collection of 2–3 digital communication assignment designs, with assessment rubrics, scaffolding sequences, and a reflective rationale that demonstrates your application of all three modules.

Part A — Digital assignment portfolio (2–3 assignments)

For each assignment: genre and format (specific), audience (named), tool choice and rationale, scaffolding sequence, and assessment rubric. These should be real assignments you intend to use — not hypothetical exercises. At least one must include an explicit accessibility requirement.

Part B — Assessment framework

Present the rubric or assessment approach for at least one of your assignments in full. Include the weight of each criterion and a holistic descriptor for at least the "Excellent" level of performance.

Part C — Reflective rationale

A 1–2 page reflective rationale explaining your design decisions. How do the three modules' frameworks — multimodal theory, digital assignment design principles, assessment strategies — inform what you built? What will you implement immediately, and what needs more development?
