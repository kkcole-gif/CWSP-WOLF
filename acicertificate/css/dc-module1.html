Foundations of Digital and Multimodal Communication

What multimodal communication is, why it matters for disciplinary instruction, and what changes — and what doesn't — when students move from print to digital genres. Estimated time: 3–5 hours over two weeks.

Section 1

What Is Multimodal Communication?

Multimodal communication draws on more than one mode of meaning-making simultaneously. A research poster combines written language, images, spatial arrangement, and color. A data visualization communicates through spatial positioning, color, and sometimes text. A recorded conference presentation adds spoken language, gesture, and real-time interaction to visual slides. Even a "traditional" academic article is multimodal — it combines linguistic, visual (layout, typography), and spatial modes.

The New London Group's 1996 manifesto, "A Pedagogy of Multiliteracies," argued that literacy education needed to account for the increasing multimodality of communication in professional and public life. Kress and van Leeuwen's social semiotic theory (1996, 2001) provided the analytical framework: each mode — linguistic, visual, gestural, spatial, audio — has distinct resources for making meaning, and skilled communicators select and combine modes deliberately.

For faculty, multimodality raises a practical question: are your students learning to communicate in the modes your discipline actually uses? A biology student who can write a strong lab report but cannot read a phylogenetic tree, interpret a gel image, or design a poster for a research symposium is missing central disciplinary literacies. A communication student who can write an essay but cannot produce a podcast, manage a social media account, or design an accessible website is similarly underprepared.

Section 2

The Five Modes

Kress and van Leeuwen's framework identifies five modes of meaning-making. Understanding them helps faculty design assignments that develop specific communication capacities, not just generic "digital skills."

Linguistic
Written and spoken language: word choice, syntax, register, genre conventions
Visual
Images, color, typography, data visualization, graphic design
Gestural
Body language, facial expression, movement — central to oral and video communication
Spatial
Layout, organization, proximity, navigational structure of digital spaces
Audio
Music, sound effects, ambient sound, vocal delivery in recorded or live contexts

Most academic writing assignments engage primarily the linguistic mode. Digital assignments engage multiple modes simultaneously — and being explicit about which modes matter in a given assignment, and why, is a form of disciplinary writing instruction that few faculty currently provide.

Section 3

From Page to Screen: What Changes, What Doesn't

Digital communication differs from print communication in ways that matter for instruction. Reading paths are less predictable online — users scan before reading, navigate non-linearly, and encounter texts in contexts (social media feeds, search results, mobile screens) that shape how meaning is made. Attention is more fragmented. Credibility signals are different — institutional affiliation and peer review work differently in digital spaces than in print. Archiving, citability, and versioning work differently. Audiences are harder to predict and often more diverse.

At the same time, the fundamentals of rhetoric — purpose, audience, context, evidence, argument — apply across print and digital contexts. The question "who is this for, what do I want them to do or think, and what will persuade them?" doesn't change when the medium changes. What changes is how those questions are answered. A data visualization makes an argument differently than a paragraph; a short-form video persuades through different mechanisms than a policy brief. Both require rhetorical thinking, but the choices are different.

A common mistake in digital assignment design

Treating digital assignments as "the same as a paper, but online." A website is not a paper published on the internet. A podcast is not an essay read aloud. A data visualization is not a paragraph with numbers. Each genre has its own conventions, and assigning one without attending to those conventions produces work that is technically digital but not actually communicating digitally.

Section 4

Digital Rhetoric and Audience

Digital rhetoric — the study of how persuasion, meaning-making, and communication work in digital environments — builds on classical rhetoric while accounting for what changes in networked, participatory, and algorithmically-mediated communication. Eyman (2015) defines it as both a practice and a field: the application of rhetorical theory to digital texts, and the study of how digital contexts produce new rhetorical situations.

Audience is the concept that shifts most significantly in digital contexts. Print genres typically address a relatively knowable, stable audience — the readers of a particular journal, the students in a particular course, the colleagues in a particular department. Digital genres address audiences that are unknown, dynamic, and shaped by platform context. A tweet can be retweeted into communities its author never anticipated. A YouTube video can reach audiences across disciplines, languages, and cultural contexts. An academic website might be read by prospective students, peer researchers, journalists, and curious undergraduates simultaneously.

For disciplinary instruction, this means teaching students to think explicitly about their intended audience, to anticipate the actual audience their digital communication will reach, and to make deliberate choices about what is sayable and how in each context.

Section 5

Digital Genres in Your Discipline

Every discipline has digital genres that practitioners use — genres as central to disciplinary communication as the research article or the lab report. Biologists produce interactive data visualizations and phylogenetic trees. Engineers create technical documentation, CAD drawings, and simulation outputs. Journalists produce multimedia packages and data-driven interactives. Historians develop digital archives and annotated editions. Public health researchers produce dashboards, infographics, and public communication campaigns.

Most faculty teach the print genres of their disciplines but not the digital ones — not because they aren't important, but because the pedagogical frameworks for digital genres haven't been as well developed. This module's activities are designed to help you identify the digital genres that matter in your discipline and begin thinking about how to teach them.

Activity 1

Multimodal Artifact Analysis

Individual analysis · 45–60 minutes

Select one digital artifact from your discipline — a data visualization, an interactive research poster, a discipline-specific website, a public-facing infographic, a recorded lecture, a social media research communication. Analyze it using the five-mode framework and the rhetorical questions from Sections 4 and 5.

Contribute to the repository

Multimodal analyses from different disciplines — especially when they identify discipline-specific digital genres — help build a cross-disciplinary map of digital communication practices at NC State.

Activity 2

Course Digital Communication Audit

Reflective analysis · 30–45 minutes

Map the digital communication demands in your current course against what you explicitly teach. This audit often reveals significant gaps — moments where students are expected to communicate digitally without instruction in how to do so.

Contribute to the repository

Course digital audits from different disciplines help CWSP understand what digital communication expectations exist across the curriculum and where support is most needed.
